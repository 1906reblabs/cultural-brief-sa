What the Oldest Festival Holds: On the Durban International Film Festival at Forty-Seven, and What South African Cinema Has Made With Its Time

The 47th Durban International Film Festival opens today at the Suncoast CineCentre. Venice is seventy-five days into its run to November. Woza Albert! enters its final three days at the Market Theatre, where it was first created forty-five years ago. The Zeitz MOCAA enters its twenty-second month without a named permanent director. This week asks what an institution owes the years it has accumulated.

"The cinema is the evening school of my people."
— Ousmane Sembène (1923–2007), filmmaker and novelist

Thursday in South Africa, and today is the opening day of the 47th Durban International Film Festival — Southern Africa's oldest, and by most measures the continent's most sustained, annual film programme. Founded in 1979 by Teddy and Ros Sarkin at the University of Natal, it opens this morning at the Suncoast CineCentre in the city of Durban, the eThekwini municipality, carrying a new curatorial leadership and a new visual frame, "The Prism of Heritage," into its fifth decade.

In Johannesburg this morning, the Market Theatre is in the final four days of Woza Albert! — Mbongeni Ngema, Percy Mtwa, and Barney Simon's 1981 protest masterwork, created in the same building where it is now playing, directed in this production by Hamilton Dhlamini with Thulani Mtsweni. The Market Theatre turns fifty this year. DIFF turns forty-seven. Both institutions were built under apartheid, both exist now in a democracy they helped make imaginable, and both have had their institutional logics — what they are for, what they owe — tested and contested at every stage of that fifty-year span.

This week's edition holds those two things in the same frame, with Venice entering its seventy-fifth day and the Zeitz MOCAA question continuing to go unanswered. The essay takes the DIFF's opening as its occasion for an institutional portrait of South African cinema — not what it has achieved (the Tsotsi Oscar, the Yesterday nomination, the international co-productions), but what it has structurally been unable to build, and what forty-seven years of festival-holding asks the sector to account for.

Also at the Market this week: the TX Theatre Hub opens today, running through Sunday, bringing five productions that were developed in whole or in part through this year's National Arts Festival fringe in Makhanda to a Johannesburg stage. It is, in small and imperfect form, exactly the kind of post-festival transfer mechanism this publication argued in July's second edition was structurally absent from the South African theatre ecosystem. That it exists, however provisionally, is worth naming.

South Africa's oldest film festival opens today in Durban, forty-seven years after it was founded, in a country that had no cinema it could freely attend at the time. The question for 2026 is not whether DIFF matters — it does — but what forty-seven years of mattering has produced in and for South African cinema. The answer is more complicated than either the festival's detractors or its defenders tend to allow.

Curated Dispatches

The 47th Durban International Film Festival — Opens Today

Durban  ·  eThekwini  ·  23 July – 2 August 2026 Opens Today

The Durban International Film Festival opens this morning at the Suncoast CineCentre, its 47th edition running through 2 August with a programme of over a hundred films spanning features, documentaries, and short films from South Africa and across the world. Founded in 1979 by Teddy and Ros Sarkin through the Centre for Creative Arts at what was then the University of Natal — now the University of KwaZulu-Natal — DIFF is Southern Africa's oldest and largest film festival and one of the oldest on the African continent. Its Best Short Film award carries automatic Oscar qualification, as does its Best Documentary award.

This year's edition is the first under the direction of Sakhile Gumede, a programmer and cultural producer who has been associated with DIFF for more than a decade. Gumede's curatorial frame — "The Prism of Heritage" — arrives with a redesigned visual identity described as taking a tech-minimalist direction. The short-film competition features twenty-four works from South Africa, the pan-African continent, and the diaspora; ten of the twenty-four are South African productions. A notable feature of this year's industry programme is a dedicated isiZulu screenwriting workshop — a formal acknowledgment, from within the festival, that the infrastructure for producing stories in South Africa's most widely spoken home language has not kept pace with the linguistic reality of the country's audiences.

Satellite screenings run at community arts centres across the greater Durban municipality, continuing the festival's long practice of extending its programme beyond the Suncoast's central venue — a practice that began under apartheid, when township screenings were one of the few mechanisms through which Black South African audiences could access the international cinema the censorship regime denied them at commercial cinemas.

47th Durban International Film Festival, Suncoast CineCentre, Durban. 23 July – 2 August 2026. Festival passes and tickets at webtickets.co.za. Industry programme and full programme details at ccadiff.ukzn.ac.za.

Woza Albert! at the Market Theatre — Final Weekend

Johannesburg  ·  Newtown  ·  Barney Simon Theatre  ·  Closes 26 July Closing Weekend

Woza Albert! closes this Sunday — 26 July — at the Market Theatre's Barney Simon Theatre, where the original work was created in 1981 by Mbongeni Ngema, Percy Mtwa, and the late Barney Simon himself. The theatre the Barney Simon Theatre is named for was one of the work's three co-creators; the irony that the room now carries his name in the building where the protest work was devised does not diminish the significance of the return. This production is performed and directed by Hamilton Dhlamini, with Thulani Mtsweni — two actors moving through the play's gallery of characters, a structure that has defined the work since its premiere and has been sustained through its Edinburgh, Berlin, Los Angeles, and international touring life across four decades.

The work's central premise — what would happen if Morena, the Lord, returned to apartheid South Africa — was, in 1981, a satirical question addressed to one of the most visibly blasphemous political systems in the world, a system that claimed Christian legitimacy while organising itself around racial terror. In 2026, forty-five years after its creation, the question lands differently: in a democracy with its own accumulated failures, in a building that is now fifty years old and has spent those fifty years insisting on what South African theatre can be when it refuses comfort. The actor-director Hamilton Dhlamini has said publicly that he regards this return as an invitation for the country to reflect on its progress more than thirty years after democracy, especially in an election year. That is not a small observation to make from the Barney Simon Theatre's stage.

The Market Theatre, Newtown, Johannesburg. Woza Albert! closes Sunday 26 July. Tickets at webtickets.co.za. markettheatre.co.za.

TX Theatre Hub at The Market — 23–26 July

Johannesburg  ·  Newtown  ·  The Market Theatre  ·  23–26 July Opens Today

The TX Theatre Hub opens today at the Market Theatre, running alongside Woza Albert! through Sunday, with five productions: Dark Magic by Masai Sepuru; Chasing Laughter by Isaac Sithole; Adam's Ale by Mxolisi Masilela; Untitled Deeds by Moruti Dlamini; and Sinking by Solly Ramatswi. Several of these productions were developed through this year's National Arts Festival fringe in Makhanda — specifically Dark Magic, Adam's Ale, and Untitled Deeds, which received strong fringe reception in Makhanda earlier this month.

This publication argued on 9 July that the South African theatre ecosystem has, in fifty-two years of festival-making, produced no reliable mechanism for transferring work from the Makhanda fringe to audiences elsewhere in the country. The TX Theatre Hub is not that mechanism — it is a curated festival partnership rather than a funded touring infrastructure — but it is a demonstration that the transfer is possible when someone chooses to make it happen. Three productions from the Makhanda fringe are currently visible to Johannesburg audiences because the programmers at The Art Cave, the Market Theatre's Kippies Fringe partner, and the TX company decided to bring them. That decision should not have been voluntary and exceptional. It should be institutional and routine. It is neither, but it happened, and that is not nothing.

TX Theatre Hub at The Market Theatre, Newtown, Johannesburg. 23–26 July 2026. markettheatre.co.za.

Venice at Day Seventy-Five  ·  Zeitz at Month Twenty-Two

Ongoing Threads  ·  Cultural Governance

The Venice Biennale Arte 2026 is seventy-five days into its run to 22 November. The critical record on "In Minor Keys" is set; the Biennale is now in the middle of its quietest period — August's attendance trough — before the return of international critical attention in September and October. South Africa's position within that record has not changed. The Goliath affair continues to be cited in serious accounts of the Biennale's political context.

The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-second month without a named permanent director. In June, the board announced the appointment of Jessica Nowitzki as a new board trustee — a US-based arts professional and philanthropist of Kenyan and Swedish descent, and a member of the Zeitz MOCAA Global Council for close to two decades. The announcement was a governance move, not a succession one, and produced no new information about the timeline or criteria for the director search. Fawaz Mustapha, Acting Executive Director and Chief Operations Officer since Koyo Kouoh's death in May 2025, continues in that role. The institution's programme continues. The succession does not.

Long Essay

What the Oldest Festival Holds: On DIFF at Forty-Seven, African Cinema's Inheritance, and What a Festival Is Still For

The Durban International Film Festival was founded in 1979. To understand what that founding meant, it is necessary to understand what South African cinema was in 1979, and what South African audiences were. The year 1979 is within the apartheid system's most consolidated period — the decade between the Sharpeville massacre and the Soweto uprising on one side, and the eventual negotiated settlement on the other. South African cinema in 1979 was, in terms of what was widely produced and distributed domestically, divided between two categories: the commercial Afrikaans-language film industry, which had grown through state subsidies from the late 1950s into a significant volume producer of populist comedies and melodramas largely consumed by white Afrikaner audiences; and the township B-pictures of the 1970s, produced quickly and cheaply, predominantly by white producers and directors for Black township audiences in venues that were themselves subject to the apartheid spatial order. International cinema reached South African audiences through the Publications Control Board, which censored and delayed an enormous volume of international film on political and moral grounds. A film that had been circulating freely in New York or London or Nairobi might reach a South African commercial cinema — if it reached one at all — a year or two later, in a cut that might bear little relationship to the director's vision.

What Teddy and Ros Sarkin founded in Durban, in that context, was something that did not obviously have a right to exist: an international film festival in a country that had not, as a matter of policy, been invited to the international conversation. The films that DIFF showed in its early years included work that the Publications Control Board would have delayed, cut, or refused outright in commercial distribution. The festival was not, as festivals sometimes are, a supplementary luxury added to a functioning film culture. It was closer to a contraband institution — one of the few mechanisms through which South African audiences, of any race, could see the world's cinema unmediated by the state's editorial apparatus.

That founding condition is not simply historical. It is the origin of everything the festival has subsequently built and failed to build, and it shapes the question that forty-seven years of continuous programming now puts to the institution: what is DIFF for when South Africa is no longer a country that needs a film festival to see films its own government has refused to permit? The apartheid state's censorship apparatus is gone. International cinema reaches South African audiences through streaming, at commercial multiplexes, and through several other festival channels that have emerged since 1979. What does the oldest festival, in this landscape, do that could not be done without it?

A festival built to circumvent censorship needs, after the censorship is gone, to explain its own continued existence. DIFF has had forty-seven years to develop that explanation. The 2026 answer is: the prism of heritage.

Sakhile Gumede's curatorial frame for this edition — "The Prism of Heritage" — is a deliberate and somewhat difficult phrase. "Heritage" in the South African context carries specific weights. It is the language of commemoration and public culture, of Heritage Day, of debates about what is preserved in museums and what is demolished and who decides. Applied to a film festival's visual identity and curatorial logic, it proposes that the festival's function is, at least in part, to hold African and South African cinema in relation to its own accumulated history — not as a retrospective institution, but as one that understands contemporary filmmaking as something that has a past, that is in dialogue with that past, and that can only be fully understood if that dialogue is acknowledged. This is a more intellectually serious claim for a festival to make than the more common one — that it is a window onto new international work, a launchpad for emerging voices — and it is worth examining whether the programme substantiates it.

The isiZulu screenwriting workshop in this year's industry programme is, in this light, the single most concrete institutional choice DIFF has made in recent years that matches its claimed function to an identifiable structural gap in South African cinema. IsiZulu is spoken as a first language by roughly a quarter of South Africa's population — the largest single home-language group in the country, larger than Zulu-speaking people in South Africa than the total population of many countries that have functioning domestic film industries. The volume of isiZulu-language cinema produced annually in South Africa is, relative to that demographic reality, almost negligible. This is not a simple question of commercial logic — Nollywood's Yoruba-language productions have demonstrated, over three decades, that vernacular cinema can find enormous domestic and diaspora audiences when the infrastructure exists to make and distribute it. It is a structural gap: South African film school curricula, funding applications, and distribution pipelines have been organised primarily in English, with some Afrikaans representation, and the development infrastructure for other languages has never been systematically built. A festival-sponsored screenwriting workshop is not a policy solution. But it is an acknowledgment, from an institution with forty-seven years of authority in the sector, that the gap is real and that acknowledging it requires more than placing a Zulu-language film in the programme once it has been made elsewhere.

What DIFF cannot do — and has never been able to do, at forty-seven years — is make South African films travel internationally. The festival is where South African films can be seen by South African and African audiences. It is not, for the most part, a primary launchpad for international distribution or major international festival selection. A South African film with serious international ambition typically needs to reach Cannes, Toronto, Berlin, or Venice independently of DIFF to find the festival exposure that generates the kind of critical record and distribution interest that sustains a film's afterlife. This is not unique to DIFF — most national and regional festivals occupy a similar structural position — but it means that the claims sometimes made for the festival's role in "putting South African cinema on the world map" require careful qualification. What DIFF does is provide South African cinema with a home audience, a critical community, and an institutional context for being seen domestically. That is not a small function, and it is not well performed by any other institution in the country. But it is a different function from the one its promotional language sometimes implies.

This distinction matters particularly in 2026, because African cinema broadly is in a moment of genuine international visibility. Films from across the continent have been finding significant places at Cannes, Berlin, and other major festivals in recent years; Nollywood's distribution revolution has demonstrated that African storytelling can reach global audiences through streaming without necessarily requiring the European festival circuit's benediction. South Africa's position within that continental moment is more ambiguous than the country's formal film infrastructure would suggest. South Africa has the largest and most technically developed film industry on the continent — more production companies, more trained technicians, more formal film school infrastructure than almost any of its neighbours. It has produced, in the three decades since 1994, a handful of internationally acclaimed films: Tsotsi winning the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film in 2006; Yesterday receiving a nomination in the same category; a number of internationally co-produced works that have circulated at significant festivals. It has not, as a sector, sustained the kind of consistent international visibility that its infrastructure and its political history — a story that most of the world, at some level, already knows — might seem to make possible.

The "Prism of Heritage" frame could be read as a way of locating South African cinema within an African tradition rather than evaluating it as a standalone national industry that has not fully broken through. That is a legitimate reframing, and it is one that the festival's satellite screenings, its focus on pan-African and diasporic work, and its commitment to supporting work that would not otherwise reach South African audiences all support. Whether Gumede's first edition as director makes that reframing productive in programme terms — whether the specific films selected, the specific industry conversations convened, and the specific development resources offered add up to something more than a well-intentioned label — is a question the next ten days will begin to answer and the next few years will need to sustain. The 47th edition is too early to judge the arc of a directorship.

What can be said on opening day is this: DIFF at forty-seven is still the oldest, largest, and most institutionally serious film festival on the continent south of the equator. It still does something no other institution in South Africa consistently does — it gathers South African and African cinema under conditions that allow it to be seen, discussed, and situated in relation to the world's filmmaking. It is still, in Sembène's register, a kind of evening school: a place where audiences learn what their own cinema has made, and where filmmakers learn what the world's cinema has been doing without them. At forty-seven, that function is less contraband than it was in 1979 and no less necessary.

Closing Reflection

In 1979, the year DIFF was founded, a Black South African without a pass book could not legally be in Durban after a certain hour. The city where the festival would build its home was a city in which the audiences most in need of seeing the world's cinema on its own terms were the audiences most systematically prevented from attending. The Suncoast CineCentre where the festival opens today did not exist in 1979. Most of what has been built since 1979 in and around South African cultural life was built in circumstances that assumed it could not or should not exist.

That assumption proved wrong. What has not yet been proved wrong is the assumption that building the festival was enough — that forty-seven years of showing films adds up, without additional institutional work, to a South African cinema capable of doing what the festival's founders presumably imagined it might one day produce. The prism of heritage is beautiful. What it refracts into is still not entirely clear.
