The Third Complaint: On Gayton McKenzie, the National Arts Council, and Eight Months of Documented Record

Heritage Month opened three days ago under the banner "Celebrating Our Living Heritage." This morning, the Democratic Alliance filed a third Public Protector complaint against Minister Gayton McKenzie — not about a single decision, but about a pattern now running from January's Venice Biennale withdrawal through the National Arts Council's dissolution and court-ordered reinstatement to this week's filing. This edition also corrects an error of its own.

Correction Signal Dispatches Long Essay Closing
“Culture is simultaneously the fruit of a people’s history and a determinant of history.”
— Amílcar Cabral, “National Liberation and Culture,” 1970

Thursday in South Africa, and Heritage Month is three days old. The Department of Sport, Arts and Culture opened it on 1 September under the 2026 theme “Celebrating Our Living Heritage: Strengthening the Ties That Bind Us.” This morning, at the Public Protector’s regional office in Hillcrest Office Park, Pretoria, the Democratic Alliance’s spokesperson on sport, arts and culture, Leah Potgieter, filed a complaint against Minister Gayton McKenzie for the third time since January.

The two facts are not unrelated, and this edition treats them as one story rather than two. Between January — when McKenzie withdrew South Africa’s independently selected submission to the Venice Biennale — and this morning’s filing sits a specific institutional record: the National Arts Council of South Africa, the statutory body responsible for distributing public arts funding at arm’s length from the department, was dissolved by ministerial notice in late May and reinstated by the Gauteng High Court at the end of July. The long essay below traces that record in full, using only what is confirmed on the public record: court findings, parliamentary committee testimony, and the Public Protector complaints themselves.

Elsewhere this week: FNB Art Joburg opens its nineteenth edition tomorrow at the Sandton Convention Centre; Iziko Museums’ in_herit programme and WOMAD Cape Town give Heritage Month its more substantive cultural content; and the two long-running threads this publication has tracked since Koyo Kouoh’s death in May last year — the Venice Biennale’s central exhibition and the Zeitz MOCAA leadership transition — both move forward this week, the first toward its November close and the second toward an October handover.

A correction precedes all of it, because it bears on one of those two threads directly.

Correction & Verification Note

Previous editions of this publication tracked the time since Koyo Kouoh’s death as a running count of “months without a director” at Zeitz MOCAA, reaching a stated twenty-fourth month by the edition of 6 August 2026. That count was wrong. Kouoh died on 10 May 2025. As of this edition, fifteen months and three weeks have passed — not twenty-four. The error compounded across several editions without being checked against the original date, and it has not been caught until now. It is corrected here, and the count restarts from the verified figure below. We are naming the error rather than quietly adjusting it, because a publication that spends this edition asking a government department to account for its own record should hold itself to no lesser standard.

Heritage Month opened this week under the banner “Celebrating Our Living Heritage.” The same week, the department that issued that banner filed its own institutional record with the Public Protector for the third time in eight months — not as complainant, but as subject. The National Arts Council was dissolved in May and reinstated by a court in July. Living heritage and institutional accountability are being asked, this week, to occupy the same sentence. They do not yet.

Curated Dispatches

Heritage Month 2026 — “Celebrating Our Living Heritage”

National Launched 1 September Heritage Day 24 September

Heritage Month opened on Tuesday 1 September under the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture’s 2026 theme, “Celebrating Our Living Heritage: Strengthening the Ties That Bind Us.” Heritage Day itself falls on 24 September — a Thursday, three editions from this one on this publication’s own calendar.

Heritage Day has been a public holiday since 1996, and has carried, since a 2005 civil-society campaign, an informal second identity as National Braai Day — a coupling that has never quite settled into official department messaging, which continues to lean toward the more encompassing “living heritage” language this year’s theme repeats. The theme’s emphasis on continuity rather than a specific historical anchor is consistent with recent years’ framing, and it arrives this year in a month where the department issuing it is separately answering to a Public Protector investigation into how several institutions in its own portfolio have been run. The official programme and the department’s institutional record are, this week, two separate accounts of the same ministry. Readers are better served attending to both than to either alone.

Department of Sport, Arts and Culture. Heritage Day, 24 September 2026. dsac.gov.za.

WOMAD Cape Town & Iziko’s in_herit — Heritage Month’s Working Programme

Cape Town WOMAD 15–24 September in_herit through 30 September

Two of the more substantive items on this year’s Heritage Month calendar are running in Cape Town across September. Iziko Museums’ in_herit programme runs across the group’s constituent museums for the full month, opened alongside Heritage Month on 1 September. WOMAD Cape Town — the local edition of the international World of Music, Arts and Dance festival format — runs from 15 to 24 September, closing on Heritage Day itself.

Both are institutional in a different sense to the department’s own campaign. Iziko is a declared cultural institution with its own board and professional curatorial staff; WOMAD Cape Town is produced independently of the department’s annual funding and messaging cycle. Their programming is set by curators and festival organisers working to their own calendars, not by ministerial announcement — which is, in a month framed around institutional continuity, not a small distinction.

Iziko Museums of South Africa, Cape Town. in_herit runs through 30 September. iziko.org.za. WOMAD Cape Town, 15–24 September. womad.co.za.

FNB Art Joburg — Nineteenth Edition Opens Tomorrow

Johannesburg  ·  Sandton Convention Centre Fair 4–6 September

FNB Art Joburg opens its main fair tomorrow at the Sandton Convention Centre, running through Saturday — the nineteenth edition of what is generally regarded as the longest continuously running contemporary art fair on the African continent. The three-week Open City programme that preceded it, distributing public-facing events across the city rather than confining them to the ticketed fair weekend, closes on 10 September.

This year’s fair introduces ARC, a new section reserved for solo presentations from galleries exhibiting at Art Joburg for the first time, alongside the fair’s established sections. The 2026 FNB Art Prize — now in its fifteenth iteration — was awarded to Thato Toeba ahead of the fair’s opening.

The fair’s continuity is, in a year when so much of the sector’s public attention has gone to institutional crisis and ministerial dispute, its own kind of fact worth naming: nineteen years of uninterrupted programming, run by a private fair organiser rather than a public entity, is not something the governance record covered elsewhere in this edition can currently claim.

FNB Art Joburg, Sandton Convention Centre, Johannesburg. Fair 4–6 September 2026. artjoburg.com.

Ongoing Threads — Venice at Day 117, Zeitz MOCAA Four Weeks From Handover

Arts Policy Ongoing

The Venice Biennale has been open 117 days of its run to 22 November. “In Minor Keys,” Koyo Kouoh’s posthumously realised central exhibition, continues to be received on the terms it established in its first month: as a formal curatorial proposition rather than a representational gesture. South Africa’s own pavilion, withdrawn from consideration by Minister Gayton McKenzie in January under circumstances now central to this edition’s essay, remains without a national submission. Gabrielle Goliath and Ingrid Masondo’s appeal against the North Gauteng High Court’s dismissal of their initial challenge remains, as far as this publication can establish, undecided.

In Cape Town, Elvira Dyangani Ose is four weeks from taking up the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa’s Artistic Director post on 1 October — fifteen months and three weeks after Koyo Kouoh’s death on 10 May 2025, not the twenty-four months this publication previously and incorrectly reported (see the correction above). Fawaz Mustapha continues as the institution’s Managing Director under the joint leadership structure announced alongside Dyangani Ose’s appointment on 12 August.

Venice Biennale Arte 2026, open through 22 November. labiennale.org. Zeitz MOCAA, V&A Waterfront, Cape Town. zeitzmocaa.museum.

Long Essay

The Third Complaint: On Gayton McKenzie, the National Arts Council, and Eight Months of Documented Record

On the morning of Thursday 3 September, at the Public Protector’s regional office in Hillcrest Office Park, Pretoria, the Democratic Alliance’s spokesperson on sport, arts and culture, Leah Potgieter, filed a complaint against Minister Gayton McKenzie for the third time in eight months. She briefed reporters afterward. It is not, on its own, a large story. Public Protector complaints against ministers are a normal feature of South African democratic life, and the DA is the official opposition; filing complaints against governing ministers is what opposition parties do. What makes the third complaint worth reading alongside the first two is not its existence but its cumulative content: read together, the three filings — January, July, and now September — describe a single, continuous record of how one department has been run since the start of this year, and that record touches almost every institution this publication has spent its life covering.

The first complaint, lodged in January, concerned a single decision. An independent selection panel had chosen South Africa’s submission to the 61st Venice Biennale: a work by the artist Gabrielle Goliath, developed with curator Ingrid Masondo, that extended its mourning to include Palestinian women and children killed in Gaza. McKenzie’s department withdrew the submission from consideration. The DA asked the Public Protector to examine whether this amounted to an improper exercise of ministerial authority over an independently curated selection. Goliath and Masondo separately challenged the withdrawal in the North Gauteng High Court, which dismissed their application; an appeal remains undecided.

The second complaint, a formal dossier submitted on 23 July, concerned money rather than curatorial content. It centred on the department’s 2025/26 Mzansi Golden Economy fund — the National Arts Council’s grant-in-aid programme for creative enterprises — and specifically on the six-member panel McKenzie appointed to adjudicate which applicants would receive it. Three of those six, the DA alleged, were active members of the Patriotic Alliance, the minority coalition party McKenzie leads, at the time of their appointment: Steve Motale, then the PA’s national spokesperson; Vernon Vraagom; and Francisco Tejada. The same dossier raised the department’s R30.9 million spend on its delegation to the 2026 Fifa World Cup in North America — R7.9 million on travel, R6.7 million on a legends exhibition match, more than R3 million on match tickets, R10 million on programme activities — a delegation the DA said exceeded the Ministerial Handbook’s limit of two support staff accompanying a minister abroad. McKenzie appeared before Parliament’s portfolio committee on sport, arts and culture on 4 August to account for the spending; the committee’s chairperson, Joe McGluwa, said the department’s presentation had lacked the financial detail members had specifically asked for. McKenzie has said his own travel cost just over R1 million across five weeks and that he declined a R55,000 subsistence allowance.

Sitting between the first two complaints and the third is a fact the DA’s dossiers reference but do not, on their own, fully explain: in late May, McKenzie dissolved the entire governing council of the National Arts Council. The council — the statutory body established to distribute public arts funding at a professional remove from the department, chaired at the time by Eugene Botha — had been in a running dispute with the minister since at least April, when McKenzie wrote to Botha over a bonus payment the council had approved. The dissolution removed all eleven sitting members. It was challenged in the Gauteng High Court, where Judge Denise Millar found, on 31 July, that the dissolution should be suspended pending a full judicial review, reinstated all eleven members, barred McKenzie from appointing a replacement council in the meantime, and ordered him to pay the applicants’ costs.

A minister who can dissolve the buffer by notice, reappoint it at will, and staff its panels with his own party has shown that its independence was never guaranteed by anything stronger than his own restraint.

The National Arts Council matters to this argument for a specific reason. It is not simply one more public entity in a minister’s portfolio. It is the body designed, in the post-apartheid arts funding architecture, to sit between the department and the artists it funds — an institutional buffer meant to keep grant decisions at a professional distance from whichever party happens to hold the ministry. That the buffer required a High Court judgment, rather than the statute establishing it, to survive a single dispute in a single month is the specific fact this year has produced, and it is a fact independent of whatever the Public Protector eventually finds.

This is the frame in which the third complaint, filed this morning, should be read. It does not concern a single decision or a single fund. According to reporting on the DA’s supplementary submission, it asks the Public Protector to broaden her investigation into whether McKenzie’s pattern of appointments — now alleged to run to at least nineteen people connected to the Patriotic Alliance across boards and directorates in his portfolio, including the National Library of South Africa, Boxing South Africa, and the National Arts Council itself — amounts to a breach of the Executive Members’ Ethics Act and the Executive Ethics Code. The submission reportedly also cites a recent Portfolio Committee meeting at which McKenzie denied that political affiliation had influenced National Arts Council appointments, and adds a new allegation concerning the World Cup delegation’s staffing under the Ministerial Handbook.

McKenzie’s public response has been consistent since January: that the complaints are politically motivated, that he has answered every question put to him in Parliament, and that no law prevents a member of a political party from serving on a public board. The Patriotic Alliance’s spokesperson, Steve Motale, has gone further, suggesting the DA is timing its complaints to land before the Public Protector, Advocate Kholeka Gcaleka, can complete her investigation ahead of November’s local government elections — an argument that, if nothing else, concedes that the timing of accountability now matters as much as its substance.

None of McKenzie’s defences is, on its face, absurd. Party membership alone does not disqualify a South African citizen from serving on a public board; a minister who answers a parliamentary committee’s general questions has, in a narrow procedural sense, discharged an obligation even if members leave the session dissatisfied with the level of detail; and a Public Protector investigation, however serious, is not itself a finding of guilt. What the record does not permit is the implicit claim that each episode is discrete and separately defensible. The Venice withdrawal, the Mzansi Golden Economy panel, the World Cup delegation, the National Arts Council dissolution, and the wider board appointments are not five unconnected stories about one minister. They are one story about what happens to public arts funding governance when the person holding the ministry treats the institutions designed to sit at a professional distance from him as extensions of his own political organisation instead.

Heritage Month’s official theme this year asks South Africans to celebrate “the ties that bind us.” The National Arts Council’s founding statute asks something narrower and more specific: that the ties binding South Africa’s public arts funding to political power be loose enough that an adjudication panel can be trusted, a pavilion selection can stand, and a council can do its work without a High Court judgment being the only thing keeping it intact. Those are not the same ties. The department currently issuing the first kind of language is the same department a court has already found, once this year, was not honouring the second. Whether the Public Protector’s investigation — assuming it concludes before November — finds anything McKenzie has not already denied in Parliament is a separate question from whether the pattern itself, now eight months and five documented episodes long, describes an institution whose independence the country can currently rely on. On the evidence assembled so far, it does not.

Closing Reflection

On 31 July, eleven people got their positions back by court order rather than ministerial goodwill. None of them asked to be dissolved in April; none of them will be thanked for enduring it. The council that decides who receives South Africa’s public arts funding now runs on the strength of a judicial review that has not yet concluded, chaired by a man the minister tried to remove, inside a department that spent tens of millions of rand on a single World Cup delegation the same year it dissolved the body meant to fund the artists who cannot afford their own flights.

Heritage Month asks the country to celebrate the ties that bind it. It does not say what happens to an institution when the only thing holding it together is a court’s willingness to say no. That question is still open. Filing a third complaint does not close it.
