The Negative: On Truth, Photography, and Who Has Held the Image in South Africa

The second Cape Town Photography Festival has taken TRUTH as its theme, in a year when images are easily manipulated. In South Africa the question has never been only whether a photograph is true. It has been who could publish it, who owned it, and what became of the people in the frame. Elsewhere this week: the Sasol New Signatures exhibition opens in Pretoria, dance from Durban reaches the Market Theatre, and this edition corrects its own record on the Venice pavilion.

Correction Signal Dispatches Long Essay Closing
…the realities of Soweto that I represent in the photographs do not necessarily translate.
— Santu Mofokeng, in Minky Schlesinger’s documentary Santu Mofokeng, quoted in the Mail & Guardian, 19 June 2026

The second Cape Town Photography Festival is in its second week. Its theme, TRUTH, was chosen by festival director Heidi Erdmann because, in her account, both truth and photography are under pressure: images can now be manipulated, recontextualised and weaponised with little effort.1 The festival has doubled in size since its first edition, to around forty exhibitions and more than 160 photographers, and has moved out of the gallery into the Castle of Good Hope, the Central Library and the Company’s Garden.2

It is easy to read that theme as a story about software. This edition reads it as a story about South Africa. The doubt now attached to every image is new in its mechanism. Its substance is not. The photographs that defined this country’s twentieth century were contested from the moment they were made: banned, seized, taken out of the country in disguise, or kept from their makers for decades. The long essay below follows that history from a single frame exposed in Orlando West on 16 June 1976 to a month-long festival on the Cape Town lawns this September.

The other three occasions in this edition are smaller and, in their way, make the same argument. In Pretoria, the Sasol New Signatures exhibition opens today with 123 works selected from 938 entries — a competition that is, among other things, a system for deciding what gets seen. At the Market Theatre in Newtown, a Durban dance festival has sent a curated selection north for four nights. In a week, the same theatre opens the South African premiere of Prima Facie, a play about what the rules of evidence do to those who must prove what happened to them.

A correction precedes the dispatches, because it concerns this publication’s own record. We are applying to ourselves the standard this edition asks of every image: where did it come from, and who can confirm it?

Curated Dispatches

Cape Town Photography Festival — Cape Rhythm in the Company’s Garden

Cape Town To 26 September Cape Rhythm opened 5 September

The festival runs from 2 to 26 September across galleries, museums and public spaces, with a public programme of conversations, workshops, a free masterclass, guided walks, film screenings and book launches.1 Some events are free; others start from R50.5 Among the programme’s elements: two exhibitions from Réunion Island; workshops from PICHA Stock, the Nairobi-based African visual-storytelling platform, which aims to move photographers from subject to storyteller and, as the platform frames it, owner; and an International Student Photography Exhibition now four times the size of last year’s.2

The exhibition worth the longest look is Cape Rhythm: Goema, Klopse & Memory, an outdoor solo show by Cape Town photographer and filmmaker Melany Ismail, curated by Erdmann, which opened in the Company’s Garden on 5 September.3 Its stated question is what it means for a culture to survive across generations, and whose histories are formally recognised, preserved and protected.4 That is an argument about archives, not only about pictures, and a public garden is a more honest place to make it than a white cube: anyone can walk past it, and it is free to leave unvisited.

Cape Town Photography Festival, venues across Cape Town, 2–26 September 2026. Cape Rhythm, Company’s Garden, Government Avenue. capetownphotofest.co.za; public programme on Quicket.

Sasol New Signatures — What 938 Entries Become

Pretoria Opens Today 10 September – 8 November

The Sasol New Signatures exhibition opens at the Pretoria Art Museum with 123 works chosen from 938 submitted at eight collection points nationwide: Bloemfontein, Cape Town, Durban, Johannesburg, Polokwane, Potchefstroom, Pretoria and Gqeberha.6 The overall winner, announced on 4 September, is Sphumelele Zondi, a 26-year-old Johannesburg sculptor and mixed-media artist entering for the first time, for The laundry of a nation. She receives R100,000 and a solo exhibition at the museum in 2027.6

The exhibition opens alongside Die Vallei van Adem, the solo show of last year’s winner, Juandré van Eck: ceramic sound vessels built to be experienced by visitors rather than only observed.6 The competition, founded some sixty years ago by the Association of Arts Pretoria, has a feature worth naming: entries are delivered in person to regional points and judged on the road, so selection begins in the regions rather than in a single city. A prize is not a system. Last week’s edition made that point about a ceremony; a competition that has run for six decades and pays its previous winner with a museum solo show comes closer to one.

Pretoria Art Museum, cnr Francis Baard and Wessels streets, Arcadia Park. Tuesday–Sunday, 10:00–17:00. One organiser page lists the opening as 11 September; most sources, including the organiser’s media release, state 10 September. Confirm before travelling. sasolsignatures.co.za.

JOMBA! @ The Market Theatre — Durban Dance, Johannesburg Stage

Newtown, Johannesburg Closing Weekend 9–12 September

The fourth JOMBA! @ The Market Theatre is a four-night, curated selection from the JOMBA! Contemporary Dance Experience, the festival run by the University of KwaZulu-Natal’s Centre for Creative Arts in Durban, which presented its 28th edition from 25 August to 6 September.7 The season opened with HEROES, from the Dutch company Introdans in collaboration with Uganda’s Joseph Tebandeke and Durban’s Flatfoot Dance Company, and honours two earlier JOMBA! Legacy Artists.7

The structure is the point. A regional festival exports a selection to a national stage on a fixed annual calendar. That is a working touring mechanism of the kind this publication’s 9 July edition argued South African theatre mostly lacks. It is also worth recording that the Market Theatre Foundation is an agency of the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture, so a state-linked venue is here hosting work generated outside the department’s own programming.7

The Market Theatre, 138 Lilian Ngoyi Street, Newtown, Johannesburg. 9–12 September 2026; full programme at markettheatre.co.za.

Prima Facie — The South African Premiere

Newtown, Johannesburg Opens 17 September To 4 October

Suzie Miller’s Prima Facie, a Tony and Olivier Award-winning play, receives its South African premiere in a How Now Brown Cow production in association with the Market Theatre, directed by Neil Coppen and starring Danica De La Rey Jones. It ran first at the Baxter Studio in Cape Town from 4 to 29 August, and transfers to the Barney Simon theatre on 17 September.8

The play follows Tessa, a barrister at the top of her profession, and examines how the burden of proof and the rules of evidence can work against victims of sexual violence.8 Coppen has said he intends to use the production to deepen discussion of its themes with audiences. Placed beside this week’s argument, it is the same question in a courtroom register: what counts as proof, who decides, and what the person asked to prove it must give up.

Barney Simon theatre, The Market Theatre, 138 Lilian Ngoyi Street, Newtown. 17 September – 4 October 2026. Wednesday–Friday 19:00; Saturday 15:00 and/or 19:00; Sunday 15:00. Tickets via Webtickets.

Long Essay

The Negative: On Truth, Photography, and Who Has Held the Image in South Africa

At half past nine on the morning of 16 June 1976, on the corner of Moema and Vilakazi streets in Orlando West, Sam Nzima of The World exposed six frames on a Pentax SL fitted with a 50mm lens.9 The third became the picture of the Soweto uprising: Mbuyisa Makhubu, eighteen, carrying the dying Hector Pieterson, with Pieterson’s sister Antoinette Sithole running beside them. The World published it the next day. Within months, the security police were pursuing the students and journalists who had been there. Nzima went home to Lillydale, where he was kept under surveillance. The World was banned in 1978. He recovered the rights to his own photograph in 1998.10

A photograph can be true and still not belong to the person who made it, or be safe for the people in it. The second Cape Town Photography Festival, running to 26 September under a single word, asks its question in the register of the present: images can be manipulated, recontextualised and weaponised.1 This essay argues that the worry is new in mechanism and old in kind, and that South African photography has always answered it with questions about control rather than about pixels.

Ernest Cole understood this before the picture left the country. To obtain the passport that let him leave South Africa with his negatives, he reclassified himself from Black to ‘Coloured’ and altered his surname from Kole to the more English-sounding Cole. The negatives became House of Bondage, published in the United States in 1967 and banned at home. When the South African embassy refused to renew his passport, he spent years living on the streets, and he died stateless in New York in 1990. His ashes were later returned and buried in Mamelodi.9 The images were true. The state’s response was to decide that their truth was not a defence.

The complication arrives from inside the tradition. Santu Mofokeng began as a photojournalist in the early 1980s and joined Afrapix, the collective founded in 1982 that encouraged photography as activism. He covered the news, but concentrated on everyday life, a subject the struggle coverage tended to pass over. In 1991, at an exhibition of those photographs at the Market Theatre, he began to ask why he was making them and for whom.11 His later conclusion, recalled in the 2026 documentary about him, was that the realities he had photographed in Soweto did not necessarily translate to people who did not live there.11 A true image needs a reader who can read it. Truth in photography is therefore a relationship, not a property of the frame.

His answer was to build a counter-archive. The Black Photo Album | Look at Me consists of 80 slides, roughly half of them reworked scans of family portraits he collected from nine families across South Africa, painterly in style and recalling Victorian studio photography.11 These were pictures Black families had made of themselves. They had not been commissioned to illustrate a political argument, and the record of apartheid had largely not kept them. Mofokeng died in 2020; the first exhibition after his death, Rumours/2026, was shown this year at the Standard Bank Art Lab in Sandton.11

The danger to a South African photograph has rarely been that no one believed it. It has been that someone else held the negative.

Set against that history, the festival’s own choices are legible. Cape Rhythm asks whose histories are formally recognised, preserved and protected.4 PICHA Stock’s stated ambition is to turn photographers from subjects into storytellers and owners.2 Erdmann reports a return to analogue film among photographers, and adds the caveat that a negative may not necessarily tell the truth either, though light physically striking film is somehow reassuring.2 That caveat is honest, and it points in the right direction. Reassurance about the image is the lesser problem. The greater one is the chain around it: who made it, who owns it, who may caption it, who preserves it, and who can prove all of this in twenty years.

That chain is infrastructure, and here the argument has to be careful. A month-long festival cannot build copyright regimes or national archives, and nothing in the programme claims to. What it can do is make the question public in places the public already uses: a library, a castle, a garden. The affordable ticket price and the free outdoor exhibition matter for that reason. They also have a limit. The festival is a month in one city, and the structures that decided Nzima’s fate — the owner of the title, the licence to publish, the sale of a newspaper group — are permanent, and operate everywhere.

The structural claim, then, is this. In South Africa the question of whether an image can be trusted has never been separable from the question of who holds it. Nzima’s twenty-two years, Cole’s passport and Mofokeng’s audience are three versions of the same problem, and generative software changes only where the weak point sits: from the image toward the system around it. A forged picture is easy to detect compared with a true one that no one is licensed to show, or that no archive was built to keep.

The unanswered question is practical. When the next image of a South African crisis circulates, who will hold its negative, who will be able to prove where it was made, and who will be paid, protected or believed?

Closing Reflection

Mbuyisa Makhubu was eighteen when he was photographed carrying Hector Pieterson. After the picture was published, the security police harassed him and he fled South Africa. He was given refuge in Nigeria. His mother told the Truth and Reconciliation Commission that she had a letter from him there and nothing afterwards. His whereabouts remain unknown.12

The photograph that identified him is among the most reproduced images of the twentieth century. It established, beyond argument, what happened on that street. It has not established what happened to him.
