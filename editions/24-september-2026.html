A Day Made of Two Claims: On Heritage Day, Shaka Day, and Who a National Holiday Is Planned For

The national Heritage Day celebration is held this morning in Oudtshoorn, the third venue named for it since 1 September. The holiday began as a compromise between a national day and a Zulu commemoration on the same date; this year the two were planned together, then separated. Elsewhere: a textile survey enters its last ten days at Zeitz MOCAA, a reference book on South African women, and a ledger of what this edition could and could not verify.

Source Ledger Signal Dispatches Long Essay Closing
Awaking on Friday morning, June 20, 1913, the South African native found himself, not actually a slave, but a pariah in the land of his birth.
— Sol Plaatje, Native Life in South Africa, 191610

The national Heritage Day celebration is held this morning at the Bongolethu Sport Stadium in Oudtshoorn, in the Western Cape. It is the third venue the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture has named for it since 1 September, when the government’s news agency announced that the day would be held at the King Zwelithini Stadium in KwaZulu-Natal, together with uMkhosi weLembe, the Zulu commemoration of King Shaka kaSenzangakhona.1 The department gives 24 September 1828 as the date of Shaka’s death.4

That coincidence of date is the reason the holiday exists in its present form. Before it was Heritage Day, 24 September was Shaka Day in KwaZulu-Natal, and every account this edition consulted agrees on the sequence: the proposed public holidays legislation left the date out, the Inkatha Freedom Party objected, and a compromise created a day for the heritage of all South Africans.7 In 2026 the department first planned to fold the two back into one event and then, after discussions with King Misuzulu kaZwelithini and the provincial MEC, separated them.4 The long essay takes that sequence as its subject. It takes no position on whether the separation was right. It asks what the sequence shows about how a day built from two claims is planned.

Other business this week sits more quietly under the same word. At Zeitz MOCAA, the first museum survey of Zohra Opoku, whose subject is cloth as inherited record, closes on 4 October, three days after the museum’s new Artistic Director takes up her post.8 A reference book on one hundred and one South African women, reviewed this month, makes a case about whose lives get recorded.9 Venice is 138 days into its run.

Zohra Opoku — We Proceed in the Footsteps of the Sunlight, Final Ten Days

Zeitz MOCAA, Cape Town Closing Soon To 4 October 2026

The first museum survey of the Ghanaian-German artist Zohra Opoku closes on Sunday 4 October. Curated by Beata America and Dr Phokeng Setai, it opened on 11 September 2025, four months after Koyo Kouoh’s death, and so was installed and has run entirely during the museum’s leadership transition.8 Opoku was born in 1976 in Altdöbern, in the former East Germany, and later moved to Accra to reconnect with her ancestral roots. She trained in fashion design and photography, and the show moves between textile installation, photography, printmaking and sculpture.8

The curatorial claim is that cloth is an active agent, shaping histories and signalling protection, in a practice about identity and inheritance. The title comes from the Egyptian Book of the Dead.8 On a day that asks the country what it holds in common, a survey whose material is inherited, hand-kept record is a fitting last look. A monograph was launched at a symposium on 6 June 2026, with an essay by Dr Greer Valley, the museum’s Senior Curator and Head of Curatorial Affairs.8

Zeitz MOCAA, Silo District, V&A Waterfront, Cape Town. Open Monday–Sunday, 10:00–18:00, last entry 17:30. Closes 4 October 2026. zeitzmocaa.museum.

101 Remarkable South African Women — A Reference Book, Reviewed

Literature Book Review Reviewed 2 September

Lizette Rabe’s reference book is on The African Imaginary’s September list, reviewed by Pheladi Sethusa and priced at R350 at The Book Lounge.9 The reviewer describes the women as complex and anchored in conviction, and notes that however controversial some may seem to a modern reader, the contexts and limited choices that shaped them become clear. She reports being ashamed at how few she recognised, particularly the historical figures, and then reads that as a consequence of the patriarchal lens through which history is recorded and taught, not a personal failing.9

The editorial interest is the form. A numbered list of one hundred and one is an act of selection, and the reviewer’s response is evidence of what a selection can recover for a reader. This edition has read one review and not the book, and the publisher and publication date were not confirmed. It is listed for the question it raises on Heritage Day, which is who does the keeping, and not as a verdict on the book.

Long Essay

A Day Made of Two Claims: On Heritage Day, Shaka Day, and Who a National Holiday Is Planned For

The Department of Sport, Arts and Culture gives 24 September 1828 as the day King Shaka kaSenzangakhona died.4 A hundred and ninety-eight years later the date is a national public holiday, and this year the national celebration of it was moved twice in three weeks. The two facts are connected, and the connection is older than the department.

In KwaZulu-Natal, 24 September was observed as Shaka Day before it was anything else. When the proposed Public Holidays Bill before the new Parliament left it out, the Inkatha Freedom Party, with a large Zulu following, objected, and a compromise was reached: a day on which all South Africans could observe their diverse cultural heritage.7 Nelson Mandela said in 1996 that the first democratically elected government made Heritage Day a national day because it knew that cultural heritage has a profound power to help build a new nation.7 The holiday was thus created as a settlement between two kinds of claim: a specific one, held by a specific people for a specific man, and a general one, held by everyone. The compromise did not choose between them. It put them on one date and left to each year’s planners the question of how they sit together.

The department’s own account of living heritage, issued in 2020, calls it the foundation of all communities and an essential source of identity and continuity, and says it is important for South Africans to reclaim, restore and preserve it.7 That is a description of something held by communities. It makes the question of who plans the day a question about the day’s subject, and not only its logistics.

The 2026 sequence is short and can be set out from the documents.

  1. 1 SeptemberThe government’s news agency reports that the celebration will be at the King Zwelithini Stadium in KwaZulu-Natal, in partnership with the Zulu Kingdom and the Royal House, combined with uMkhosi weLembe. Media accreditation closes on 10 September.12
  2. Before 21 SeptemberAn advisory says the President will lead the celebration at Rosemoor Stadium in Thembalethu, George. The accreditation deadline is extended to 21 September.3
  3. Before 24 SeptemberThe Minister announces Oudtshoorn, at the Bongolethu Sport Stadium, and says the KwaZulu-Natal event will have its own day after discussions with King Misuzulu kaZwelithini and the provincial MEC.4
  4. 24 SeptembereNCA reports the President will lead the event at Oudtshoorn, and that the Western Cape’s Cultural Affairs MEC, Ricardo Mackenzie, criticised the last-minute changes and said affected municipalities were not properly consulted.5

Both halves of the decision are stated in the department’s advisory. The Minister said uMkhosi weLembe belongs to the Zulu nation and deserves its own stage. The same advisory records sympathy for the businesspeople of George who would have benefited from hosting, and says George will still get its national day, with no date given.4

A holiday made to hold two claims is held, in practice, by whichever claim has someone in the room when the venue is chosen.

This essay does not adjudicate. A separation agreed with the king can be defended as respect for a specific claim. A national day that rested wholly on one kingdom’s commemoration can be argued the other way. The sources contain neither argument in full. What they establish is narrower. In the documents this edition could read, the venue decisions were taken between the Ministry, the royal house and the KwaZulu-Natal MEC. The Western Cape’s MEC says the host municipalities were not properly consulted. And the heritage bodies and practitioners that the month’s own programme names, in intergenerational dialogues, indigenous knowledge symposia and museum education, do not appear in the venue decisions at all.3

That is the structural point, and it is a small one. In the first years of the democratic Parliament the room was a legislature with a party objecting inside it. In 2026 it was a ministry, a king and a provincial minister. Neither process is illegitimate. Both leave the day’s other claimants, the places that host it, the practitioners who keep what the theme calls living heritage, and the public, to learn the outcome from an advisory.

The theme says what the department wants the day to promote: national unity, social cohesion, Ubuntu and a shared sense of belonging.3 A process that was quick, bilateral and announced afterwards sits uneasily beside those words. Whether it is unusual is a different question, and this edition cannot answer it. It did not find how venues were chosen in earlier years, so it cannot say whether 2026 was worse than any other. Last year Oudtshoorn hosted at the Bridgeton Sport Grounds under a theme about heritage institutions; this year it hosts at a different ground under a theme about living heritage.6 The venue changes. The explanation, each time, is given afterwards.

Sol Plaatje opened his account of the 1913 Land Act with a sentence about waking to find oneself a pariah in the land of one’s birth.10 It is quoted here for the structure, not the comparison: a law was made, and its subjects learned its meaning once it was in force. The 2026 decision is a smaller thing, and nothing in the sources suggests intent. But the question the sentence puts, who is told and who is asked, applies to any day the state makes in the name of everyone.

What the word “national” attaches to in a national day remains open: a place, a house, or a date. The date has not moved. The sources do not say when George’s national day will be held, or who will decide.

Closing Reflection

The date in every advisory this edition read is 24 September. It is the only element that did not change between 1 September and today. The department attaches it to a death in 1828, and the theme attaches it to belonging, Ubuntu and social cohesion.

In three weeks the national celebration had three addresses: a stadium named for a Zulu king, a stadium in Thembalethu, and a stadium in a Klein Karoo town that hosted it last year. No document read for this edition says when George’s national day will be held.
