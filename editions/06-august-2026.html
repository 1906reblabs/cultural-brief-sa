The Rock and the Elegy: On the Seventieth Anniversary of the 1956 Women's March, Gabrielle Goliath's Unfinished Mourning, and What Constitutional Protection Has Actually Delivered

Sunday marks seventy years since twenty thousand women marched on the Union Buildings and left sixty thousand petition signatures at the Prime Minister's door. National Women's Day falls on the day itself this year, with Monday observed as the substituted public holiday. Venice is eighty-nine days into its run, in its quietest month. The Zeitz MOCAA enters a twenty-fourth month without a named director. This week returns to where this publication began in March — to Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy — and asks what, seventy years on, has actually changed.

Signal Dispatches Long Essay Closing
"Wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo — you strike a woman, you strike a rock."
— Sung by the marchers, Union Buildings, Pretoria, 9 August 1956

Thursday in South Africa, three days before the seventieth anniversary of the day twenty thousand women — Black, white, Coloured, Indian, organised by Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa, and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn — walked in silence to the Union Buildings and left sixty thousand signatures against the extension of pass laws to women. National Women's Day falls on the anniversary itself this year, a Sunday, which means Monday is the substituted holiday and the country will observe the day at one remove from its own occasion, in the manner this publication has by now noted often enough to call a pattern.

The song the marchers sang that day has outlasted every government that has tried to claim it since. It is printed on t-shirts and read into parliamentary record and, this week, it opens an edition of a cultural newsletter, which is its own kind of institutional afterlife — the slogan surviving so completely that the specific danger it names can start to feel abstract. This edition tries not to let that happen. It returns, deliberately, to the work this publication opened with in its first edition on 5 March: Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy, the polyphonic mourning-work for women killed by the men who were supposed to love them, cancelled from the Venice pavilion in January, shown anyway in an alternative Venice space in May, still touring the argument it was always making.

Venice itself is eighty-nine days into its run this week, deep in the Biennale's quietest month before the return of international attention in September. The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters a twenty-fourth month without a named permanent director — a fact this publication has now tracked across nine consecutive editions. Neither of those threads resolves this week. What this week does instead is ask a harder, more specific question of the anniversary itself: whether the constitutional and institutional protections built in the thirty years since 1994 have caught up to the promise the 1956 marchers made on behalf of the women who would come after them.

The essay this week takes up that question directly, using Elegy as its test case and the seventieth anniversary as its occasion. Below: what the country is doing with the anniversary, what Venice's quiet month is preparing for its return, where the Zeitz succession currently sits, and where Durban's oldest festival landed after its closing weekend.

Seventy years ago this Sunday, twenty thousand women stood in silence at the Union Buildings for thirty minutes because the Prime Minister would not receive them. The pass laws they marched against are gone. The women they sang about — the ones a hand cannot be laid on without striking a rock — continue to be killed by the men in their own homes at a rate the state has not meaningfully reduced. The rock held. The protection it was meant to guarantee is still being built.

Curated Dispatches

National Women's Day at Seventy — Pretoria and the National Programme

Union Buildings, Pretoria  ·  9 August 2026 Advance Notice

National Women's Day falls on Sunday, on the seventieth anniversary of the 1956 march itself — the first time in some years the observed date and the historical date have coincided exactly. The national commemoration returns, as it does most years, to the Union Buildings amphitheatre in Pretoria, with the customary presidential address and the reading of names that has, in recent years, expanded to include the names of women killed in the preceding twelve months — a practice introduced by civil society organisations and now, unevenly, adopted into the official programme.

The Lilian Ngoyi statue on Lilian Ngoyi Street in Johannesburg and the Sophiatown heritage precinct will both host smaller, locally organised commemorations that this publication's audience — culturally serious readers with an interest in how memory is built at street level rather than only at the podium — may find more useful than the Union Buildings programme itself. Sophia Williams-De Bruyn, the youngest of the four march leaders, was seventeen in 1956; she is the only one of the four still living, and her presence, when it is confirmed at any commemoration, remains the most direct living thread between 2026 and the day itself.

Monday 10 August is the substituted public holiday. For readers planning cultural attendance across the long weekend: most major institutions will observe Sunday hours on the public holiday itself.

Union Buildings, Pretoria. Sunday 9 August 2026 (observed Monday 10 August). Presidential address time to be confirmed via gov.za.

"What the Body Remembers" — Standard Bank Gallery, Johannesburg

Johannesburg  ·  Opens 8 August 2026 Opens This Weekend

The Standard Bank Gallery opens a Women's Month group exhibition on Saturday, bringing together painting, textile, and performance-documentation work by a cross-generational group of South African women artists under the title What the Body Remembers. The curatorial premise is specific rather than commemorative: that the body holds a form of historical record that official archives do not, and that a significant strand of South African women's art practice — from the ghoemaliedjies tradition through to contemporary performance work — has always understood memory as something carried physically rather than only documented.

The exhibition includes new textile work responding directly to the uNomkhubulwane cosmology this publication examined in relation to Nduduzo Makhathini's music in March — the Zulu rain deity understood as a regulator of fertility and nature, here reframed by a visual artist as a figure of protection rather than only abundance. It is a curatorial connection worth noting on its own terms: the same cosmological register that animated a jazz record in March is, five months later, animating a very different body of work, in a very different medium, without either artist having coordinated the choice.

Standard Bank Gallery, Simmonds Street, Johannesburg. 8 August – 27 September 2026. Free entry. standardbankarts.co.za.

Zeitz MOCAA — Twenty-Fourth Month

Cape Town  ·  V&A Waterfront Institutional Governance

The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-fourth month without a named permanent director. There is, this week, a genuine if minor development to report: unconfirmed reporting in the trade press suggests the board has narrowed an informal shortlist to a small number of candidates, at least two of whom are women with senior curatorial experience at African or diasporic institutions. This publication has not been able to verify the reporting independently, and the board has issued no public statement confirming or denying it.

If accurate, the shortlist would represent the first forward movement in a process this publication has tracked, without variation, across nine consecutive editions. It would not, on its own, constitute the public timeline and criteria disclosure this publication has argued the institution owes the sector it serves. A rumoured shortlist is not an announced process. The distinction matters, and this publication will not treat unconfirmed reporting as resolution until the board itself says so.

Zeitz MOCAA, V&A Waterfront, Cape Town. Open Tuesday–Sunday. zeitzmocaa.museum.

Venice at Eighty-Nine Days — The Quiet Before September

Arts Policy  ·  Venice Biennale Ongoing

The Venice Biennale has been open eighty-nine days, deep in the August attendance trough that separates the preview-season critical rush from the September and October return of international press and collectors. "In Minor Keys" continues to run at the pace the quiet month allows — smaller weekday crowds, longer engagement times reported by gallery attendants, the kind of viewing conditions the exhibition's atmospheric, unhurried curatorial logic was arguably built for in the first place.

What August is preparing for is visible in the Biennale's own late-year programming announcements: a September symposium series at the Arsenale is confirmed to include a panel specifically addressing gender-based violence in contemporary African performance practice, with Gabrielle Goliath named among the confirmed participants — her first formal platform inside the Biennale's own programming architecture, six months after the ministerial cancellation that kept her out of the official pavilion altogether. The irony is not subtle, and this publication will not pretend it is.

Venice Biennale Arte 2026. Open through 22 November 2026. September symposium series details at labiennale.org.

Durban International Film Festival — After the Closing

Durban  ·  Closed 2 August 2026 Closed

The 47th Durban International Film Festival closed last Sunday after eleven days at the Suncoast CineCentre and its satellite venues. Early attendance figures released by the Centre for Creative Arts show a modest increase over the 2025 edition, concentrated disproportionately in the satellite township screenings rather than the central venue — a pattern the festival's organisers have described as encouraging and this publication would describe as the festival's oldest and most durable achievement finally showing up in the numbers.

The isiZulu screenwriting workshop flagged in this publication's opening-day dispatch produced, by the festival's own account, eleven completed treatment drafts from participants who had not previously written in the language for a commissioning context. Whether any of those eleven drafts finds funding is a question that will take considerably longer than a festival's eleven days to answer, and one this publication intends to return to.

47th Durban International Film Festival. Closed 2 August 2026. ccadiff.ukzn.ac.za.

Long Essay

The Rock and the Elegy: What Seventy Years Have Actually Delivered

On 9 August 1956, roughly twenty thousand women walked to the Union Buildings in Pretoria and stood in silence for thirty minutes, because Prime Minister J.G. Strijdom had refused to receive their delegation or their petition. The petition carried more than one hundred thousand signatures, organised by the Federation of South African Women under the leadership of Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa, and Sophia Williams-De Bruyn, protesting the extension of the pass laws — the documents that controlled Black South Africans' movement through their own country — to women for the first time. The silence, when it broke, became a song: Wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo. You strike a woman, you strike a rock. It is not a boast. It is a warning, sung by people who had just demonstrated, in the most literal possible sense, that they could not be moved.

Seventy years later, on the anniversary itself, the pass laws are gone. Women vote, hold office, sit on the Constitutional Court, run companies and universities and, until relatively recently, one of the country's largest cultural institutions. The formal architecture of exclusion the 1956 marchers were protesting has been dismantled by three decades of constitutional and legislative work that the marchers themselves could not have specified but plainly made possible. This is real, and this publication has never treated it as anything other than real. What this essay is interested in is a narrower and harder question: whether the protection the song promised — the rock that does not move when struck — has been built into the institutions that were supposed to carry it forward, or whether it remains, seventy years on, primarily a song.

Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy is the specific case this publication has tracked since its first edition, and it remains the clearest available test of the question. Elegy is a polyphonic performance work, built around female opera singers, mourning South African women killed by intimate partners and Namibian women killed in the Herero and Nama genocide. Its 2026 iteration proposed extending that mourning to Palestinian women and children killed in Gaza, including a tribute to the poet Hiba Abu Nada. In December 2025, Minister Gayton McKenzie wrote to the pavilion's producing organisation describing the Gaza component as "highly divisive," and by January the selection had been cancelled outright — twice, across two successive governance models, both of which collapsed under the same underlying refusal to let the work be shown on its own terms.

A rock does not need to be struck twice to prove it has not moved. That the state required two attempts to remove the same work is not evidence of the rock's strength. It is evidence of how much force it takes to hold the position, and how little the position has cost the people applying the force.

The North Gauteng High Court dismissed the constitutional challenge to the first cancellation without written reasons. The Gauteng High Court dismissed the second challenge on contractual grounds, again without engaging section 16's protection of artistic creativity on its merits. Both courts had the opportunity to determine, definitively, what the constitution actually guarantees an artist whose work a minister finds inconvenient. Both declined. What is left, seventy years after the march, is a work about the killing of women that the state twice refused to fund, that a court twice declined to protect on constitutional grounds, and that now exists — as Goliath said it always could — because she found somewhere else to put it. The rock held. It held despite the institutions built to hold it, not because of them.

This is not a small distinction, and it maps directly onto the broader statistical reality the 1956 march's legacy is measured against every August. South Africa's femicide rate is among the highest recorded outside active conflict zones, and has not fallen meaningfully across the period in which the country's constitutional protections for women were being built. The government's own National Strategic Plan on Gender-Based Violence and Femicide, adopted in 2020, set implementation targets that successive progress reports have acknowledged the country is not meeting, particularly on the funding and staffing of the specialised courts and shelters the plan itself identified as essential. The gap here is structurally identical to the gap this publication has tracked in the cultural sector for five months: a right that exists in the founding document, and an infrastructure that has not been built at the scale the right requires to be more than a proclamation.

What connects the femicide statistics to the Goliath case is not simply that both concern violence against women. It is that both reveal the same institutional pattern: a state that will proclaim the value of women's safety, and women's cultural expression, in its founding documents and its August speeches, while consistently underfunding, under-litigating, or actively suppressing the specific mechanisms that would make the proclamation real. A minister who cancels a mourning-work about femicide, in the same year his department's own strategic plan on femicide falls short of its targets, is not a contradiction. He is the pattern working exactly as the pattern works: proclamation at the podium, retreat at the point of implementation, and a persistent gap between the two that the institutions responsible for closing it have not closed.

The Zeitz MOCAA succession, tracked across nine editions of this publication without resolution, belongs in the same frame, though the connection is structural rather than thematic. Koyo Kouoh built the most significant institutional argument South African contemporary art has produced in a decade, and the institution she built has spent twenty-four months unable to name a successor in public. That two of the reportedly shortlisted candidates are women — unconfirmed, unannounced, exactly the kind of institutional silence this publication has spent five months arguing against — is not an accident worth celebrating prematurely. It is, at best, the shortlist stage of a process that the board has still not been willing to make public, seventy years after twenty thousand women stood in silence specifically because the institutions of the day would not receive them.

What would it mean, in the cultural sector specifically, to build the rock the 1956 marchers sang about into actual institutional practice? It would mean pavilion governance structures with explicit, litigable limits on ministerial content interference — the exact reform this publication has argued for since March, and that neither of the two collapsed Venice processes produced. It would mean courts willing to rule on section 16's merits rather than retreating to contractual grounds when a constitutional question about women's mourning is squarely before them. It would mean a Zeitz MOCAA board willing to disclose its process rather than let unconfirmed trade reporting do the work of accountability the board itself has declined to do. None of these are radical proposals. They are the ordinary infrastructure of institutional seriousness, and their absence, seventy years after the march, is the actual measure of the gap between the song and the state.

Elegy will be shown again, formally, inside the Venice Biennale's own programming architecture in September — not in the pavilion the state refused to build, but in a symposium the Biennale itself convened. Goliath will speak about gender-based violence in contemporary African performance practice on a stage the ministry that cancelled her had no hand in constructing. That is not vindication. It is the same pattern this publication named in March: the work exists because its maker refused to let the state's refusal be the final word, encountered by exactly the audience the state's own platform would have reached, without the state's platform. The rock held. Whether the institutions meant to protect it will ever hold it themselves is the question the seventieth anniversary poses and does not answer.

Closing Reflection

South African researchers have, for years, cited a femicide rate that amounts, on the most widely used estimates, to a woman killed by an intimate partner roughly every few hours — a figure disputed in its precise arithmetic but not in its order of magnitude, and not, in any single year since the estimate first circulated, meaningfully improved. Seventy years ago this Sunday, twenty thousand women stood silent at the Union Buildings for half an hour because a prime minister would not see them. The silence was itself the argument: we are here, and we are not moving, and you will have to explain to history why you would not open the door.

The door has, in the formal sense, opened. Women hold the offices the marchers were barred from. What has not yet opened, seventy years later, is a reliable guarantee that the women the song was sung for will be protected once they are inside — whether that protection takes the form of a court willing to rule, a ministry willing to fund its own strategic plan, or a board willing to say, in public, who it has chosen to lead. The rock has held for seventy years because the people who are struck keep choosing not to move. That is not the same as the institutions having built anything to catch them.
