Five Hundred Copies: On Jazz Epistle, Verse 1, Kippie Moeketsi, and Who Kept the Record

In January 1960 six musicians recorded the first full-length LP by a Black South African jazz ensemble, and five hundred copies were pressed. Seven days before Heritage Day, a saxophonist prepares a tribute to the group’s alto player, and a painter opens her first museum solo in Cape Town. This week asks who has kept the record, and what the answer says about heritage.

“There had never been a group like the Epistles in South Africa.”
— Hugh Masekela, Still Grazing: The Musical Journey of Hugh Masekela, 20043

Seven days from today the country takes a public holiday called Heritage Day, the last Thursday of a month the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture has framed as “Celebrating Our Living Heritage.” Two things are already open that suit the phrase better than most of the official programme. On Tuesday evening, Norval Foundation in Tokai opened Kate Gottgens’s first museum solo. And on 25 and 26 September the 27th Standard Bank Joy of Jazz convenes at the Sandton Convention Centre, with a tribute to the saxophonist Kippie Moeketsi among its programme.

Moeketsi is this edition’s subject, approached through an object rather than a biography. In January 1960 he and five other musicians went into the Gallo studio in Johannesburg and recorded Jazz Epistle, Verse 1. Five hundred copies were pressed.1 Two months later came Sharpeville and the repression that followed, and the band broke up. The long essay asks a plain question about that record: who has kept it, and on what terms.

The longer threads move this week, if slowly. Venice is 131 days into a run that closes on 22 November, sixty-six days from now. The Goliath appeal has a development: according to a Daily Maverick report published after this edition’s date and marked as such below, the Johannesburg artist’s application for leave to appeal was heard on 8 September and granted.13 And Elvira Dyangani Ose takes up the Artistic Director post at Zeitz MOCAA in fourteen days, sixteen months after Koyo Kouoh died on 10 May 2025.

Curated Dispatches

Kate Gottgens — Unsteady Realms, Norval Foundation

Tokai, Cape Town Opened 15 September To 7 February 2027

Norval Foundation opened Unsteady Realms in its Gallery 1 on Tuesday, curated by Carmen Joubert, running to 7 February 2027. The museum describes it as the first time a museum has given Gottgens a solo exhibition, bringing together new and existing paintings.12 Gottgens was born in Durban in 1965, graduated from the Michaelis School of Fine Art at the University of Cape Town in 1987, and lives in Cape Town. Her first solo show was at Everard Read in Johannesburg in 1993; her work has since been shown in Antwerp, Brussels, Knokke, New York and London.12

The editorial point lies in her method. Her gallery in Knokke describes compositions built from other people’s images: anonymous snapshots found at flea markets, family holiday photographs, fragments from the internet’s archive, stripped of context and reconstructed.12 That is a practice about found images and the lives attached to them, and it sits close to the question this publication followed last week through the Cape Town Photography Festival. The museum gives the artist her own account of the figures: “holograms of potential,” both life-giving and life-destroying. A walkabout with Gottgens takes place on Saturday 19 September at 11:00.12

Norval Foundation, Steenberg Road, Tokai, Cape Town. Unsteady Realms, 15 September 2026 – 7 February 2027. Artist walkabout Saturday 19 September, 11:00. norvalfoundation.org.

Standard Bank Joy of Jazz — The Tributes

Sandton, Johannesburg Advance Notice 25–26 September

The 27th Joy of Jazz runs on Friday 25 and Saturday 26 September at the Sandton Convention Centre, across four stages, with doors at 17:00 on Friday and 16:00 on Saturday. Angélique Kidjo and Kamasi Washington headline; the programme also lists Nubya Garcia, McCoy Mrubata, Judith Sephuma, Nduduzo Makhathini and the 2026 Standard Bank Young Artist winners Gabi Motuba and Manana. Day passes start at R1,150 and weekend passes at R2,250 through Ticketmaster.11

This publication does not cover corporate programming as such, and the festival carries its sponsor in its name. The reason it is here is the shape of the programme. Three tributes to musicians who have died are scheduled: Thandiswa Mazwai to Hugh Masekela; Buhlebendalo Mda, Zawadi YaMungu and Xolisa Dlamini to Busi Mhlongo; and Khaya Mahlangu to Kippie Moeketsi, which the festival places on the Dinaledi Stage.11 A tribute set is a form of custody, and the question of who performs the custody is the subject of this week’s essay. The organisers say the festival has grown from 1,800 attendees in 2000 to more than 25,000.

Peter Esterhuysen’s Kippie Moeketsi: Sad Man of Jazz — A Book and Its Title

Literature  ·  Retrospective note Not a new title

This is a note on an existing book, not a launch. Peter Esterhuysen’s Kippie Moeketsi: Sad Man of Jazz was published by Viva Books in 1995, according to the bibliography entry in the standard references, and both the South African History Online entry and the Presidency’s national-order citation for Moeketsi cite it as the account of his life.978 This edition has not been able to confirm whether it is in print.

The title is the editorial matter. A 2025 centenary article by a writer who interviewed South African musicians reports that all of them dismissed the picture of a sad and occasionally mean drunk as irrelevant to the man they had known.6 A 2019 account of Johannesburg’s Newtown precinct records a musician objecting to the plaque beside Moeketsi’s bronze statue, on the grounds that the “sad man” narrative is tiring.10 The same centenary article attributes the epithet to Moeketsi’s playing on Sad Times, Bad Times from the 1959 jazz opera King Kong.6 A biography fixes a name in public memory, and readers of this one should hold the title as an interpretation. The most recent centenary reissue, Hard Top (1976) from As-Shams in 2025, offers the music without the epithet.

Peter Esterhuysen, Kippie Moeketsi: Sad Man of Jazz, Viva Books, 1995. Availability unconfirmed. Moeketsi, Hard Top, 1976; reissued by As-Shams Archive, 2025.

Long Essay

Five Hundred Copies: On Jazz Epistle, Verse 1, Kippie Moeketsi, and Who Kept the Record

In January 1960, at the Gallo studio in Johannesburg, six musicians recorded Jazz Epistle, Verse 1: Kippie Moeketsi on alto saxophone, Hugh Masekela on trumpet, Jonas Gwangwa on trombone, Dollar Brand, later Abdullah Ibrahim, on piano, Johnny Gertze on bass and Makaya Ntshoko on drums. It was the first full-length LP by a Black South African jazz ensemble. Five hundred copies were pressed.12 Two months later, on 21 March, police killed sixty-nine people at Sharpeville, and the state hardened. “We had to break up,” Masekela later said of the band.2

The accounts differ on detail. One places the recording in 1959; most place it in January 1960.4 They agree on the number. Five hundred is a small figure even by the standards of a segregated record industry: the country’s first bebop band left behind a pressing that would fill a single hall. Where the sources part is on why. One 2017 account says the recording was buried by the regime. The better-documented claim is narrower: after Sharpeville the government treated jazz groups with suspicion and targeted them, and the Epistles dissolved.14 This essay does not claim to know how the pressing was handled once it existed. It claims only what the record’s size and date make plain.

A record is a decision about who is expected to listen. Five hundred copies in 1960 assumed a small audience and made no provision for a long afterlife. What gave the music one was the work of other people, in an order that is worth setting out. The first custodian was the musicians. Moeketsi taught Ibrahim, who by one account walked from Cape Town to Johannesburg to study with him, and the Presidency’s citation credits him with mentoring the pianist.28 Ibrahim and Masekela left South Africa in the early 1960s, and the group’s music travelled in their later careers.1 On 27 April 2017, Freedom Day, Ibrahim and his Ekaya ensemble played Verse 1 at Town Hall in New York, with Lesedi Ntsane on trumpet in place of Masekela, who had been unable to travel.4

The second custodian was the small label. A vinyl reissue from the Adventure in Sound imprint appeared on 25 August 2014, collecting selections from Verse 1 with other cues the band had recorded for the American pianist John Mehegan in 1959 and 1960. Its retailer notes that few copies of the original survive, without giving a count.5 In the centenary year of Moeketsi’s birth, another independent label, As-Shams, reissued his 1976 solo album Hard Top, the most recent of a run of reissues that, in the words of one article, made his short-run recordings newly accessible.6

The state appears in the sources later and in a different register. Moeketsi died in 1983. The South African History Online entry says he died like a pauper, as many South African musicians did.7 The Presidency has since published a citation for him in a national order, awarded posthumously for his contribution to the development and popularity of jazz in South Africa and its integration into the genre internationally.8 That citation gives his birth year as 1924. South African History Online, Wikipedia and a 2025 centenary article give 27 July 1925.67 The discrepancy is small and worth noting for what it shows: the institution that honours the record has not settled the date on which the man was born.

A tribute set is a form of custody. So is a reissue, a statue and a citation. Each decides what is kept, and none of them is neutral about it.

This is the structural claim, and it should be stated with its limits. In the documents this edition located, the work of keeping Moeketsi audible is done by musicians, by two small labels and, next weekend, by a saxophonist at a festival that carries a bank’s name. The state’s contribution that the documents show is a citation. That is a statement about the sources gathered here and says nothing about every archive in the country. Where the documents are silent, this essay does not assume absence. It records that heritage, in this case, travelled through private hands: the record’s makers, its reissuers and its sponsors.

There is nothing disreputable in that route, and some of it has served the music well. A label that reissues a 1976 album in 2025 has done a job no ministry has been asked to do. But the route has properties that a public archive would not. A label answers to collectors, and a sponsor answers to a brand. Neither is obliged to keep a piece of music available once the interest passes. The epithet attached to Moeketsi shows the cost of leaving the story to biographers and plaque-writers: the musicians who knew him dispute the name that has stuck, and the plaque outside the former Kippies club in Newtown carries the narrative one of them finds tiring.10

Heritage Day asks the country each year to claim a living heritage. The question this case puts to the claim is concrete: when a musician’s work depends on five hundred copies, a few reissues and an annual tribute, who is responsible for what happens in the years when no one is paying attention? Joy of Jazz will answer for one weekend, and the answer will be good. The record’s makers and reissuers have answered for decades.

The sources do not say how many of the five hundred survive, who holds them or where. The 2014 retailer says only that fewer survive than were made. No institution in the documents gathered here has published a count.

Closing Reflection

Jeremiah Morolong Moeketsi was a boy when his mother went looking for him among the mine dumps, calling “kippie, kippie, kippie,” to take him back to school. The call became his name. It is now the name of a bronze figure in Newtown, of a club the city knows as the old Kippies, and of a tribute that a saxophonist will lead in Sandton next weekend.

School did not keep him. Five hundred copies of one record reached few of the people who might have heard it. Many people have answered the call since, in many forms. None of the sources says what it was asking him to come back to.
