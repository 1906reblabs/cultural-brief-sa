The Room and the Resort: On the Masque Theatre, Sun City, and What Thirty Years of the Constitution Has Learned to Applaud

Tonight, a 174-seat theatre in a converted Muizenberg bowling alley opens three nights of new South African writing. Tomorrow, the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture hands out its National Arts and Culture Awards at Sun City, in a year it has chosen to frame around thirty years of the Constitution. This week asks which of the two rooms the country actually built for culture — and which one it chose for the ceremony.

Signal Dispatches Long Essay Closing
"I've always sensed for myself an obligation to bear witness to my time."
— Athol Fugard (1932–2025)

Thursday in South Africa, and two rooms open today. In Muizenberg, a theatre built in 1959 inside a derelict bowling alley opens its One-Act Play Festival — three nights of new South African writing, developed over months of readings and feedback, performed by four playwrights most of the country has never heard of. In Johannesburg, FNB Art Joburg's Open City programme opens across the inner city and its northern suburbs, the first three weeks of a civic prelude that ends at the Sandton Convention Centre in September with the continent's longest continuously running contemporary art fair. Tomorrow, in North West province, the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture convenes its National Arts and Culture Awards at Sun City, under the banner "Celebrating Our Nation's Creative Excellence," broadcast the following evening on SABC1.

Three cultural occasions, three registers, three rooms. This edition holds them side by side because the third happens to sit inside a building whose history the other two illuminate by contrast. Sun City opened twenty years after the Masque, in 1979, in the nominally independent Bantustan of Bophuthatswana, built specifically so that entertainment apartheid law forbade inside South Africa's own borders could still be sold to the country's white population a short drive away. Within six years it became the most recognisable target of the global cultural boycott — the resort a generation of the world's most significant musicians publicly refused to enter. That the state's flagship celebration of the nation's creative excellence now convenes there, in the year the department has chosen to frame around the Constitution's thirtieth year, may be no more than a booking department's indifference to history. This edition thinks it is worth looking at directly rather than past.

Venice has been open 103 days of its run to 22 November. Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy, shown independently at the Chiesa di Sant'Antonin from early May through the end of July after its removal from the national pavilion, is now confirmed for Ibraaz in London this October; the appeal against the pavilion's cancellation remains undecided more than six months after it was filed. In Cape Town, six weeks remain before Elvira Dyangani Ose takes up the Zeitz MOCAA directorship on 1 October, and the one piece of public commentary to follow last week's announcement — from the Mail & Guardian — asked plainly why the museum's fifteen-month search produced no South African finalist. The museum's board has not answered.

The long essay this week takes up the Masque and Sun City as its argument. The dispatches carry the rest of the week's real business.

Tonight, a room that seats under two hundred people opens three nights of plays nobody has seen before. Tomorrow, the department responsible for the country's cultural policy hands out its national awards in a casino built to dodge apartheid's own gambling and morality laws, twenty years after that first room began letting Black and white South Africans sit in the same seats. Both are real. Only one of them required nobody's permission.

Curated Dispatches

The Masque Theatre — One-Act Play Festival Opens Tonight

Muizenberg, Cape Town  ·  20–22 August 2026 Opens Today

The Masque Theatre in Muizenberg opens its One-Act Play Festival tonight, running through Saturday: three evenings built around four new South African plays that have spent months moving through the theatre's development pipeline — submission, staged reading, audience and peer feedback, rewrite, full production — before reaching the stage this week. Tonight's bill pairs Clive Myburgh's The Black Bird, directed by Kerith Coulson, with Bottle Service; Friday evening and Saturday afternoon carry An Unexpected Guest and Lady Marmalade, from playwrights Kudzai Mhangwa, Lindy Wilson and Savannah Steyn; Saturday evening repeats tonight's pairing. Each performance closes with a post-show discussion between the playwrights, directors and cast, produced by Faeron Wheeler.

The festival is unglamorous by design, and that is the point worth making this week. The Masque has no marketing budget, no corporate sponsor, no national broadcast slot. What it has is a process — submission to reading to feedback to full production, repeated with enough consistency across enough years — that lets a playwright with no institutional backing anywhere in the country watch a finished version of their own script performed to a paying audience within a single season. That is closer than it should be to the exception rather than the rule. It is also, this publication argued after the National Arts Festival's fringe closed last month in its 9 July edition, precisely the mechanism South African theatre does not reliably have: a way of carrying new work from the page to a stage that was not simply rented for the week.

The Masque Theatre, 37 Main Road, Muizenberg. 20–22 August 2026. Tickets from R150 via Quicket.

FNB Art Joburg — Open City Opens Today

Johannesburg  ·  Open City 20 August – 14 September  ·  Fair 4–6 September Opens Today

FNB Art Joburg's Open City programme begins today, three weeks before the fair itself opens at the Sandton Convention Centre — a civic prelude, run in partnership with Brand South Africa, that distributes the fair's public-facing programming across the city rather than confining it to a single ticketed weekend. The Rand Club, the exclusive Johannesburg institution built for the mining-era establishment, opens its doors to Mary Sibande and Lawrence Lemaoana's Occupying the Gallery; Goodman Gallery continues Mikhael Subotzky's A Johannesburg Landscape through the end of October; the BMW Art Generation Vol. IV showcase for emerging artists runs the last weekend of August. Open City closes on 14 September, by which point the main fair — now in its nineteenth year, the longest continuously running contemporary art fair on the African continent — will already have opened and closed at Sandton, from 4 to 6 September.

This year's fair adds a new section, ARC, dedicated to solo presentations from galleries showing at Art Joburg for the first time — WORLDART, Graham Contemporary and White River Gallery among the debut exhibitors — alongside the seven returning sections that have organised the fair's identity for years. More than a hundred artists and exhibitors from nineteen African countries are expected. The 2026 edition also arrives in the year Johannesburg marks a hundred and forty years since its 1886 founding, a coincidence the organisers have not been shy about naming. Whether a mining-camp city turned financial capital owes its contemporary art fair anything by way of civic reflection, or whether the anniversary is simply a convenient number, is a question Open City's choice of venues — the Rand Club chief among them — seems to be asking without quite stating.

FNB Art Joburg, Sandton Convention Centre, Johannesburg. Fair 4–6 September 2026. Day passes from R180. artjoburg.com.

National Arts and Culture Awards — Sun City, Tomorrow

Sun City, North West  ·  21 August 2026  ·  Broadcast SABC1, 22 August Advance Notice

The Department of Sport, Arts and Culture holds its third National Arts and Culture Awards at Sun City tomorrow evening, under the theme "Celebrating Our Nation's Creative Excellence," with the ceremony broadcast on SABC1 the following night. Twenty competitive awards are distributed across six categories — Heritage Site, Visual Arts and Crafts, Design and Creative Services, Performing Arts, Audio Visual and Interactive Media, and Literature — alongside a separate set of Ministerial Awards. Pamela Mtanga and Vafa Naraghi host; confirmed performers include Zee Nxumalo, Holly Rey, Yanga Chief, Tu Nokwe, Brenden Praise and Jimmy Nevis. Nominees for Poet Performance include Koleka Putuma, Masai Sepuru and Modise Joshua Sekgothe; the Theatre Production shortlist includes Breakfast with Mugabe, Verwelkingslied and The Fatherland.

The department's own advance framing situates the ceremony against two other 2026 anniversaries: thirty years since the Constitution, and fifty years since the introduction of television broadcasting in South Africa. Neither anniversary is incidental to a ceremony whose sponsor list includes Super Group, Metropolitan, AMKA and Yamaha, and whose broadcast partner is the same public broadcaster that spent much of the apartheid era refusing airtime to the kind of Black cultural production the NACAs now exist to honour. What the department has not addressed in its advance materials for this ceremony is the sector's continuing account of its own handling of this year's Venice Biennale selection — the same department, the same year, a different room. The long essay below takes up the venue question directly.

National Arts and Culture Awards, Sun City, North West. Friday 21 August 2026. Broadcast SABC1, Saturday 22 August, 20:00. dsac.gov.za.

Arts Policy Ongoing

The Venice Biennale has been open 103 days of its run to 22 November. Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy, shown independently at the Chiesa di Sant'Antonin from early May through the end of July after its removal from the national pavilion, is now confirmed to travel to Ibraaz in London this October. The appeal against the North Gauteng High Court's February dismissal of Goliath and Ingrid Masondo's urgent application — a dismissal decided on contractual rather than constitutional grounds — remains undecided, with no ruling publicly reported more than six months after it was filed. South Africa's Arsenale pavilion space has stood empty since the Biennale's public opening on 9 May, the first time the country has failed to participate since 2011.

In Cape Town, six weeks remain before Elvira Dyangani Ose takes up the Zeitz MOCAA Artistic Director post on 1 October, fifteen months after Koyo Kouoh's death. The one piece of public commentary to emerge since the appointment was confirmed on 12 August, covered in this publication's 13 August edition, came from the Mail & Guardian, which noted plainly that the museum's fifteen-month search produced no South African finalist, and suggested the question of why would not go away quietly given the country's current political mood. The museum's board has not responded publicly.

Long Essay

The Room and the Resort: On the Masque Theatre, Sun City, and What Thirty Years of the Constitution Has Learned to Applaud

On 10 January 1959, a Cape Town attorney named Bertie Stern opened a theatre in a derelict bowling alley on Main Road, Muizenberg, that he had bought two years earlier and rebuilt largely with his own money. He seated it for fewer than two hundred people. He called it the Masque. In the Cape Town of 1959 — a city already three years into the Group Areas Act's reorganisation of who could live, work and gather where — Stern's theatre and one other, the Old Space, were the only two venues in the city where Black and white performers and audiences could occupy the same room. This was not a loophole the law had overlooked. It was a choice the theatre's founders kept making, production by production, at a moment when making it carried consequences.

The Masque did not become a national institution. It survived, which in South African cultural infrastructure is its own kind of achievement. Stern ran it until his death in 1993, then left it in trust to four independent amateur dramatic societies, who have governed it collectively ever since through a management committee with no paid executive and no state subsidy. A fire gutted the building in 1997; the philanthropist Joan St Leger Lindbergh helped fund its rebuilding, and the theatre reopened in 1999. It nearly closed during the 2020 lockdowns, kept alive by a public appeal; it appealed again in 2025, for a leaking roof and a crumbling wall. Athol Fugard performed on its stage in the years before his name carried the weight it would later carry, alongside Yvonne Bryceland, Janet Suzman and Percy Sieff. None of this made the Masque famous. It made it, instead, a working piece of infrastructure — sixty-seven years old this year, still run by volunteers, still doing the one thing it was built to do.

What the Masque was built to do is legible in this week's One-Act Play Festival, which is not, on its own terms, a significant cultural event. Four new plays, three nights, a room that seats under two hundred people. But the mechanism behind it — submission, staged reading, structured feedback, rewrite, full production, all inside a single season, all without requiring the playwright to already have an agent, a university affiliation, or a prior production credit anywhere in the country — is precisely the mechanism this publication argued, after the National Arts Festival's fringe closed last month, that South African theatre does not reliably have. The NAF's fringe lets anyone with a lease agreement stage work in Makhanda for eleven days and does very little to guarantee what happens to it afterward. The Masque's process is smaller, slower and unglamorous, and it produces the thing the larger, more visible apparatus does not: a finished script, tested in front of a paying audience, that did not require its writer to already be known.

Twenty years after Stern opened his theatre, on 7 December 1979, the hotel developer Sol Kerzner opened a very different kind of room. Sun City was built in the nominally independent Bantustan of Bophuthatswana — a territory the apartheid state had declared a sovereign nation and no other government on earth recognised as one — for a specific commercial reason: gambling and topless revue entertainment were illegal in South Africa proper, and Bophuthatswana's fictional independence meant neither was illegal there. Kerzner built a casino and entertainment complex two hours from Johannesburg and Pretoria that let white South Africans do, a short drive from home, what apartheid's own morality legislation forbade them from doing inside the republic. Black South Africans, meanwhile, faced serious restrictions on entering the resort at all.

Sun City did not stay a domestic curiosity. In 1985, the musician Steven Van Zandt organised more than two dozen prominent international performers — among them Bob Dylan, Bruce Springsteen, Miles Davis, Peter Gabriel and Run-DMC — into a recording and public campaign built around one specific refusal: they would not perform at Sun City. The record they released became one of the most widely recognised cultural artefacts of the global anti-apartheid movement, and Sun City became, for the rest of the decade, the resort a generation of the world's most visible musicians were publicly known for refusing to enter. Some performers broke the boycott anyway — Frank Sinatra, Elton John, Rod Stewart and Queen among them — and were placed on a United Nations register of sanctions-breakers for doing so. The refusal and the breach were both, in their own way, a measure of how much the venue had come to mean.

The department did not have to choose Sun City. That it chose Sun City, in the year it has chosen to frame around the Constitution's thirtieth anniversary, is either an oversight nobody in the planning process thought to flag, or a fact nobody thought worth flagging.

None of this makes Sun City today the same place it was in 1985, and a fair account has to hold that complexity rather than flatten it. Sun International has run the resort since well before 1994; it has hosted South African cultural, sporting and civic events across three decades of democracy, largely without the kind of scrutiny this essay is applying to it now. Buildings outlive the specific political purposes that built them, and a country that insisted every apartheid-era structure carry its history forward permanently, unusable for anything else, would not have much infrastructure left to use at all. The Company's Garden is a Dutch East India Company provisioning station. The Artscape was built to exclude the audiences it now serves. This publication has argued, in both cases, that transformation of use is possible and worth recognising when it happens.

What is different about Sun City this week is not the venue's history in the abstract. It is the specific coincidence of occasion: a ceremony explicitly framed by the department that runs it as marking thirty years of the Constitution — the founding document built, in its most basic terms, to dismantle exactly the architecture of manufactured "independence" and racial exclusion that Sun City's existence depended on — held inside the physical structure that architecture produced, in a resort a generation of the world's most significant artists once refused, on principle, to enter. North West province has other venues capable of hosting an awards ceremony. That the department chose this one, in the year it has chosen to frame around the Constitution's thirtieth year, is either an oversight nobody in the planning process thought to flag, or a fact nobody thought worth flagging. Neither possibility reflects especially well on how seriously the anniversary is actually being held.

The Masque Theatre asked nothing of the state to keep functioning for sixty-seven years beyond being left alone by the segregation laws it quietly ignored from its first production. It has no marketing budget, no televised evening, no ministerial address. What it has produced, consistently, without subsidy, is the mechanism this country's theatre sector has spent the past two months arguing it structurally lacks: a way for an unknown writer to see a finished version of their own work performed. Sun City asks nothing of the state this week either — it is simply the venue, booked for an evening, for a ceremony about creative excellence that the department has itself connected to thirty years of constitutional order. The Masque did the harder thing quietly, for free, since 1959. The department's ceremony will be free to watch on television, staged in a room built specifically to be free of exactly the constraints the Constitution exists to reimpose. Both are part of what this country has built with its culture. Only one of them required anybody to have thought carefully about where it was happening.

Closing Reflection

Athol Fugard performed on the Masque's stage before Cape Town or the world had much reason to know his name. He said, once, that he had always sensed an obligation to bear witness to his time. He died in March last year, seventeen months before this week's four unknown playwrights took the same stage he once stood on, in the same unglamorous room, in front of an audience that paid R150 to be there.

Tomorrow, a different kind of witnessing happens at Sun City, on a different kind of stage, watched by however many million people SABC1's Saturday evening reaches. Both count as this country's cultural record this week. The Constitution turns thirty this year. It has not yet made clear which of the two rooms it was actually built for.
