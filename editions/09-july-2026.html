After Makhanda: On Touring Infrastructure, and What Happens to the Work When the Festival Ends

The National Arts Festival closed Sunday. Four days on, the church halls are empty and the sets are in storage or in skips. Venice has been open sixty-one days. The Zeitz MOCAA enters its twenty-first month without a named director. This edition asks the question the closing weekend cannot answer on its own: what does South Africa's cultural infrastructure do with the work that Makhanda produced, now that Makhanda is finished with it?

"Without forgiveness, there is no future."
— Desmond Tutu, No Future Without Forgiveness, 1999

Thursday in South Africa, four days after the National Arts Festival closed in Makhanda. The eleven days are over. The fringe venues have been swept out and handed back to the university and the churches that lent them. The performers who spent June and early July building sets in borrowed halls have driven home, most of them to Johannesburg and Cape Town, a handful further afield, carrying with them whatever the festival gave them: a review, a booking enquiry, a conversation with a programmer from another city, or nothing at all beyond the fact of having made the work and had it seen by whoever happened to be in the room.

The Tutu line above is not about the festival directly. It is about what a country does with what has already happened — the discipline of not letting an event close and become simply the past, but insisting on what it obligates going forward. Reconciliation, in Tutu's usage, was never a single ceremony; it was the ongoing labour of carrying a reckoning past the moment it was named. The National Arts Festival's closing weekend is a much smaller occasion, but it poses a structurally similar question: whether the country's cultural sector treats the festival as a discrete, closed event, or as an obligation that continues once the tents come down.

This week's long essay takes up that question directly, using the specific case of a National Arts Festival with no reliable mechanism for what happens to a fringe production after its final performance in Makhanda. Venice remains open, at sixty-one days and steady in its critical reception. The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-first month without a named permanent director, a fact this publication has now tracked across seven consecutive editions without variation in the outcome. And at the Market Theatre in Newtown, Lara Foot's fiftieth-anniversary commission has opened and can, for the first time, be assessed rather than previewed.

None of these stories resolve this week. That is, increasingly, the honest condition of South African cultural life in the middle of 2026: not a sequence of dramatic events but a set of slow, unresolved processes — a board's appointment, a festival's afterlife, an exhibition's closing arc — that this publication continues to hold in view because holding them in view is, itself, a form of the labour Tutu was naming.

The National Arts Festival closed Sunday. What travels forward from Makhanda — which productions get remounted, which performers get called back, which company's next funding application is read differently — will be decided in the months ahead, by institutions that have no obligation to decide well. The festival's job was to let the work exist. Whether the work continues to exist is somebody else's unassigned responsibility.

Curated Dispatches

National Arts Festival — The Standard Bank Ovation Awards, and What They Do and Do Not Settle

Makhanda, Eastern Cape  ·  Closed 5 July 2026

The Standard Bank Ovation Awards, announced in the festival's final days, named the Forgotten Angle Theatre Collaborative's three-generational land piece — flagged in this publication's advance coverage three weeks before it opened — among the fringe's standout productions, alongside two solo works this edition previewed without naming. The awards are a useful record of what the festival's own critical apparatus judged to matter most. They are not, and have never claimed to be, a comprehensive account of everything that was worth attending to across roughly three hundred fringe productions.

What the Ovation Awards reliably do is confirm the concentration this publication described last week: recognition accrues to work that already had visibility — an established company, a returning name, a subject legible to the critics assembled in the room during the festival's first weekend. This is not a criticism of the judging; it is a structural description of what any awards process operating inside a single eleven-day window can achieve. The awards close the loop on the productions that were already being talked about. They do not, and structurally cannot, surface the ones that were not.

For readers who did not attend: the recorded citations and the adjudicators' comments, when the festival publishes them, are the most useful available record of this year's fringe — considerably more useful than any single review, and worth reading in full rather than in excerpt.

National Arts Festival, Makhanda. Ovation Award citations at artsfestival.co.za.

Market Theatre — Lara Foot's New Commission, Reviewed

Newtown, Johannesburg  ·  Fiftieth Anniversary Season

Lara Foot's new commission for the Market Theatre's fiftieth-anniversary season opened last week and has now run long enough to be assessed rather than merely previewed. The production continues the formal conviction that has organised Foot's work for three decades: that the small, precisely observed story carries within it the structural conditions that produced it, and that theatre's task is to make those conditions visible without translating them into a register more comfortable for audiences unfamiliar with the specific history involved.

What distinguishes this commission within the anniversary season is its refusal of the retrospective register that round-number anniversaries typically invite. Rather than looking back at what the Market Theatre has been, the work looks at a specific, contemporary situation — a family negotiating the disposal of a deceased parent's flat in a Johannesburg suburb undergoing rapid demographic change — and lets the historical weight of the building's transformation accumulate without being named directly. This is the harder version of anniversary programming: a new work that earns its place in the season on its own terms, rather than one that simply gestures backward at the institution's history.

Early critical response has been strong, and specifically strong on the performances rather than the writing, which is the more interesting register for a Foot premiere, whose scripts are typically praised first. Whether the production sustains its run at the level of its opening week is the question worth returning to before the season closes.

Market Theatre, Newtown, Johannesburg. Season programme and tickets at markettheatre.co.za.

Zeitz MOCAA — Twenty-First Month

Cape Town  ·  V&A Waterfront  ·  Institutional governance

The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-first month without a named permanent director. This publication has tracked the succession question across seven consecutive editions, and the pattern each time has been identical: no public timeline, no public process description, no public account of the criteria under consideration. Venice's opening did not change the pattern. The fiftieth anniversary of June 16 did not change the pattern. The National Arts Festival's closing — the country's most sustained annual demonstration of what South African cultural production can do when it is given the conditions to work — has also not changed it.

The institution's curatorial programme continues, sustained by staff who worked alongside Koyo Kouoh and who understand the intellectual project she built well enough to keep it operating without a name at its head. That continuity remains real and should not be discounted. But twenty-one months is no longer a transitional period in any ordinary institutional sense. It is the condition the institution now operates under, and the longer it continues without public accounting, the more it resembles a decision rather than a delay.

Zeitz MOCAA, V&A Waterfront, Cape Town. Open Tuesday–Sunday. zeitzmocaa.museum.

Venice at Sixty-One Days — Approaching the Exhibition's Second Half

Arts Policy  ·  Venice Biennale  ·  Open through 22 November 2026

The Venice Biennale has been open sixty-one days, roughly a third of its full run. The critical record on "In Minor Keys," which formed within the exhibition's first month, remains stable: the exhibition continues to be received as a formal proposition about what a Biennale central exhibition can be, organised around affinity and atmosphere rather than geographic representation, and the reception has not drifted back toward the more familiar language of diversity and inclusion that early coverage of African-curated exhibitions has historically settled into.

South Africa's position in that record has not changed and is unlikely to change before November: a case study in the distance between cultural intelligence and political governance, cited in serious accounts of the exhibition as context rather than footnote. Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy, shown in an alternative Venice space during the preview period, continues to circulate in critical writing about the Biennale's periphery — evidence that the work found its intended audience regardless of the pavilion's emptiness, which was always Goliath's own account of what the work required.

Venice Biennale Arte 2026. Open through 22 November 2026. labiennale.org.

Long Essay

After Makhanda: On Touring Infrastructure, and What Happens to the Work When the Festival Ends

On 5 July 2026, the National Arts Festival closed. By the following Tuesday, the fringe venues in Makhanda — the church halls, the university studios, the converted shopfronts that had, for eleven days, been performance spaces — had been swept out and returned to their ordinary institutional lives. The productions that had run in them, some to full houses and strong word-of-mouth, most to smaller audiences that thinned as the festival's attention concentrated elsewhere, no longer exist as functioning productions. The sets are in storage, if the maker could afford storage, or in skips if they could not. The cast has dispersed. What remains, for the overwhelming majority of what was staged in Makhanda this year, is nothing more than the memory of the people who happened to be in the room.

This is not a new observation about the National Arts Festival. It is the festival's oldest and least resolved structural feature, present since 1974 and unaddressed by fifty-two years of otherwise serious institutional development. The festival has built, over five decades, one of the more sophisticated infrastructures for producing new South African theatre anywhere in the world: a fringe model that lowers the barrier to entry for makers without institutional backing, a main programme that curates and resources ambitious commissioned work, an adjudication system that generates critical attention within the festival's own window. What it has never built, and shows no sign of building, is any mechanism for what happens to the work after the eleven days end.

Compare this to the infrastructure that exists, imperfectly but functionally, in other national theatre ecosystems. The Edinburgh Fringe — the model the NAF's own fringe structure is loosely descended from — operates inside a broader British and international touring circuit in which producers, venues, and festival programmers from other cities actively scout Edinburgh for work to book elsewhere. A production that does well at Edinburgh has, at minimum, a plausible pathway to a life beyond the festival: a transfer to London, a slot at a subsequent festival, an international tour booked on the strength of reviews generated during the festival's run. This pathway is imperfect and itself concentrates attention on already-visible work, but it exists as institutional infrastructure, built and maintained by touring companies, national funding bodies, and venue networks that understand festival exposure as the first stage of a longer process rather than the whole of it.

South Africa has no equivalent infrastructure at anything like the same scale. There is no national touring circuit that reliably picks up NAF fringe standouts and moves them to Johannesburg, Durban, Cape Town, and the smaller regional centres that would benefit from seeing them. The Market Theatre, the Baxter, the State Theatre, and the handful of other serious venues with programming budgets operate largely independently of each other, each making its own decisions about what from Makhanda is worth booking, with no shared calendar, no coordinated funding mechanism, and no institutional memory that persists from one year's festival to the next. A production that impresses adjudicators and critics in Makhanda in July has, in practice, only the informal networks of its own maker to rely on for what happens next: a phone call to a venue manager who happened to see the show, a mention at an industry gathering, the accumulated professional relationships of whoever wrote or directed the piece.

This absence is not for lack of institutional will in any simple sense. The National Arts Council, whose governance crises this publication has documented across multiple editions, does not have a stable enough funding pipeline to underwrite a national touring programme even if the political will to build one existed. The provincial arts councils that might, in a better-resourced system, coordinate regional touring have themselves been subject to the same cycles of funding instability. The result is a cultural ecosystem that excels at production — at getting new, ambitious, formally serious work made and staged, in Makhanda and at the handful of other festivals and venues that support new work — and fails almost completely at circulation. South Africa makes more interesting new theatre per year, relative to its resources, than most comparable middle-income countries. Very little of it is seen by more than the small number of people who happened to be in the specific room where it was first staged.

The consequence of this failure compounds in a specific and measurable way: it privileges makers who already have institutional relationships, because in the absence of a systemic touring infrastructure, the only reliable mechanism for a production's afterlife is a maker's personal network of programmers, funders, and critics who already know their work. This is precisely the inequality the fringe's low barrier to entry is supposed to counteract. The fringe lets anyone with a lease agreement stage work in Makhanda. What happens after Makhanda reintroduces, through the back door, exactly the institutional gatekeeping the fringe was designed to bypass.

What would touring infrastructure require? Not, in the first instance, large amounts of new money — though money would help. It would require, at minimum, a shared calendar among the country's serious venues, so that a programmer at the Baxter knows what a programmer at the Market Theatre saw and rated in Makhanda, and vice versa. It would require a modest transfer fund, administered independently of any single venue's programming budget, that could underwrite the specific costs of moving a set and a small company from Makhanda to Johannesburg or Cape Town — costs that are frequently the only thing standing between a strong festival reception and an actual second life. And it would require the will, on the part of at least some of the country's arts journalists and critics, to follow up on what they praised in July rather than moving immediately to the next festival, the next opening, the next occasion.

None of this is architecturally complicated. It is politically and institutionally neglected, in the specific way that infrastructure projects without an obvious ribbon-cutting moment are neglected by funding bodies and government departments that measure success in terms of single events rather than sustained systems. A minister can be photographed opening a festival. No minister is photographed maintaining a touring fund that quietly moves productions between cities for a decade. The absence of glamour in infrastructure work is, perversely, one of the reasons infrastructure work does not get built.

Desmond Tutu's insistence that reconciliation is not a single ceremony but an ongoing labour applies, at a much smaller and more mundane scale, to what this country's cultural sector owes the work it produces. The National Arts Festival's closing weekend was, in its own register, a kind of ceremony — awards given, citations read, a festival concluded in the manner festivals conclude. What comes after the ceremony, in Tutu's framework, is the actual obligation: the sustained, unglamorous labour of carrying what was named forward into a future that does not resolve itself automatically. South Africa's cultural sector has built, over fifty-two years, an impressive capacity to produce the ceremony. It has built almost no capacity to carry out the labour that the ceremony, on its own, cannot discharge.

The productions that closed in Makhanda on Sunday are, in the overwhelming majority of cases, already gone — not destroyed, but returned to the private possession of the people who made them, with no institutional mechanism reliably available to bring them back into public view. This is not a scandal in the way a ministerial cancellation is a scandal. It is quieter and more structural than that, and for exactly that reason it is more likely to persist unaddressed. A cancelled pavilion generates outrage. An absent touring fund generates nothing at all, which is precisely the condition under which it continues, year after year, not to be built.

Closing Reflection

Somewhere in Johannesburg or Cape Town this week, a performer who staged a well-reviewed fringe production in Makhanda is back at whatever work pays the rent between commissions, waiting to see whether the festival's attention translates into anything further. In most years, for most makers, it does not. There is no institution whose job it is to make sure it does. The festival gave the work eleven days and a room. What happens next was never anyone's responsibility to guarantee, which is exactly the arrangement that needs to change and shows no sign of changing this year either.
