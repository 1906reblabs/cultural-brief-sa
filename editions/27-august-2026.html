What Fifty Thousand Rand Buys: On the National Arts and Culture Awards, and the Difference Between a Prize and a System

Mikhael Subotzky opens a new exhibition in Johannesburg today. FNB Art Joburg's Open City programme enters its second week, with the BMW Art Generation weekend two days off. Venice is a hundred and ten days into its run. Elvira Dyangani Ose is five weeks from Cape Town. This week asks what a cheque, handed out once a year on television, actually repairs — and what it does not.

Thursday in South Africa, six days after twenty South African artists stood on a stage at Sun City and were handed fifty thousand rand apiece. The National Arts and Culture Awards were held on Friday 21 August, broadcast the following evening on SABC1, under the theme "Celebrating Our Nation's Creative Excellence" and a creative concept the department called "Going Way Back" — a phrase meant to reach for seventy years since the 1956 Women's March, fifty since the Soweto uprising, thirty since the Constitution, and fifty since South African television first went to air. It was, by the department's own account, the first year winners received money instead of a certificate.1 Minister Gayton McKenzie was direct about why: artists had been complaining for thirty years, he said, and complaints do not pay rent.

In Johannesburg, this morning, Mikhael Subotzky opens A Johannesburg Landscape (or Thirteen Tons Settled to Brick) at Goodman Gallery — a show built, in part, around his own family's arrival in the city, and around the specific, almost absurd fact that a great-grandfather fleeing Nazi Germany paid his way into a Johannesburg address with a thirteen-tonne steamroller.4 Two kilometres away, FNB Art Joburg's Open City programme is a week into its run across the inner city and the northern suburbs, with the BMW Art Generation weekend — featuring performances from Thando Zide and Nduduzo Makhathini, whose cosmological jazz this publication examined in its second edition in March — opening on Saturday, ten days ahead of the fair itself at Sandton.

Venice has been open a hundred and ten days of its run to 22 November. Gabrielle Goliath and Ingrid Masondo's appeal against the dismissal of their challenge to the cancelled Venice pavilion selection remains, as far as this publication can establish, undecided — roughly six months after the notice to appeal was filed and opposed. In Cape Town, Elvira Dyangani Ose is five weeks from taking up the Zeitz MOCAA directorship. Neither thread moves this week. Both remain worth naming, because the money question at the centre of this edition's essay is the same question that sits underneath each of them: what an institution is actually resourced to do, as distinct from what it is willing to announce.

This week's essay takes the NACA cash prizes on their own terms — genuinely welcome to the people who received them — and asks what they do and do not answer, set against a National Arts Council this publication has tracked through governance crisis, a touring infrastructure this publication has documented as structurally absent, and a museum that has spent much of this year without a named head. Below: the full account of who was honoured and what changed, the Subotzky opening, the FNB Art Joburg week, and where Venice and Zeitz currently stand.

Twenty artists were paid fifty thousand rand each at Sun City last week. The department that paid them has, by its own minister's account, been hearing the same complaint about money for thirty years. A prize answers a complaint once. It does not build the thing that would stop the complaint recurring. Both facts are true of the same evening, and neither cancels the other out.

Curated Dispatches

The National Arts and Culture Awards — What the Money Bought

Sun City, North West Held 21 August Broadcast SABC1, 22 August

The Department of Sport, Arts and Culture's National Arts and Culture Awards were held at Sun City on Friday 21 August, presented by Pamela Mtanga and Vafa Naraghi, with performances across the evening including Yanga Chief and Mawhoo. Twenty competitive category winners each received fifty thousand rand — the first time a NACA win has carried a cash prize rather than a certificate — with non-winning nominees receiving twenty thousand rand each.1 Winners named across the categories included Themba Ndaba, Koleka Putuma (for the poetry work Water), Gregory Maqoma, David Tlale, Zenande Mfenyana, Sisa Benjamin Zako and Mellaney Roberts, among others.2

Separate Ministerial Awards, several carrying their own cash recognition, went to Leon Schuster and Alfred Ntombela for lifetime achievement, and — posthumously — to Miriam Makeba, Don Mattera, the musician Mandoza (Mduduzi Tshabalala, whose family received a further hundred thousand rand), and the late HHP (Nkosinathi Maphumulo). Felicia Mabuza-Suttle, Rebecca Malope and Alec Khaoli were also honoured. The ceremony's sponsors included Super Group Dealerships, JETOUR, Metropolitan, AMKA and Yamaha, and it was followed on Sunday by the department's National Sports Awards at the same venue.2

Don Mattera's line — "memory is the weapon" — opened this publication's edition on 4 June. Miriam Makeba's words opened the edition of 13 August. Neither posthumous honour was known to this publication when those quotes were chosen. The overlap is worth noting rather than reading into.

National Arts and Culture Awards, Sun City. Held 21 August 2026; broadcast SABC1, 22 August. dsac.gov.za.

Mikhael Subotzky — A Johannesburg Landscape (or Thirteen Tons Settled to Brick)

Johannesburg Opens Today 27 August – 31 October

Mikhael Subotzky opens a new body of work at Goodman Gallery in Johannesburg today, running through 31 October. Subotzky — born in Cape Town in 1981, a Magnum Photos member whose work sits in the collections of the Museum of Modern Art and the Centre Pompidou — has built the show in part around his own family's arrival in the city: a great-grandfather who fled Nazi Germany, arrived via the Woermann shipping line, and is understood to have paid his way to settling in Johannesburg with a thirteen-tonne steamroller, the fact the exhibition's title carries forward.4

The work extends Subotzky's long-standing practice of physically cutting and reassembling his own photographs — landscape treated as something built from disassembled parts rather than captured whole, a method that reads, in this show, as a formal argument about how a city like Johannesburg gets settled in the first place: brick by brick, arrival by arrival, often by means as unglamorous as a secondhand steamroller.

Goodman Gallery, 163 Jan Smuts Avenue, Parkwood, Johannesburg. 27 August – 31 October 2026. goodman-gallery.com.

FNB Art Joburg — Open City's Second Week, BMW Art Generation This Weekend

Johannesburg Open City to 14 September Fair 4–6 September

FNB Art Joburg's Open City programme — the civic prelude to the fair itself, run in partnership with Brand South Africa and distributed across the inner city and northern suburbs — enters its second week. The BMW Art Generation Vol. IV showcase for emerging artists opens this Saturday, 29 August, with performances from Thando Zide and Nduduzo Makhathini — the pianist and sangoma whose Blue Note album uNomkhubulwane was the subject of this publication's second long essay in March.

The fair proper opens in one week and a day at the Sandton Convention Centre, running 4–6 September — its nineteenth year, and by most accounts the longest continuously running contemporary art fair on the African continent. More than a hundred artists and exhibitors from nineteen African countries are expected, alongside a new section, ARC, dedicated to solo presentations from galleries showing at the fair for the first time. Organisers have connected this year's edition to Johannesburg's hundred-and-fortieth anniversary since its 1886 founding.5

FNB Art Joburg, Sandton Convention Centre, Johannesburg. Open City to 14 September; fair 4–6 September 2026. artjoburg.com.

Ongoing Threads — Venice at Day 110, Zeitz Five Weeks Out

Arts Policy Ongoing

The Venice Biennale has been open a hundred and ten days of its run to 22 November. Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy, shown independently at the Chiesa di Sant'Antonin in Venice's Castello district from 5 May through 31 July after its removal from the national pavilion, is confirmed to travel to Ibraaz in London this October with the support of the Bertha Foundation.6 The appeal brought by Goliath and curator Ingrid Masondo against the North Gauteng High Court's dismissal of their urgent application — a dismissal made on contractual rather than constitutional grounds, with reasons only made public four days after the ruling itself — remains undecided, as far as this publication can establish, roughly six months after Minister McKenzie's legal team filed notice to oppose it.6 South Africa's Arsenale pavilion space has stood empty since the Biennale's public opening on 9 May, the first time the country has failed to participate since 2011.

In Cape Town, Elvira Dyangani Ose is five weeks from taking up the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa's Artistic Director post on 1 October, fifteen months after Koyo Kouoh's death. No further public statement has followed the museum's 12 August announcement.

Long Essay

What Fifty Thousand Rand Buys: On the National Arts and Culture Awards, and the Difference Between a Prize and a System

On the evening of 21 August 2026, at a resort built in a fabricated Bantustan to let apartheid South Africa gamble on the wrong side of its own morality laws, twenty South African artists were called up one by one and handed cheques for fifty thousand rand. It was, according to the department that wrote the cheques, the first time in the National Arts and Culture Awards' history that a category win had come with money rather than a certificate. Minister Gayton McKenzie, presenting the change as his own initiative, put the reasoning plainly: for thirty years, he said, artists, designers and people behind the camera had been complaining, and nothing shows a government's interest in someone the way giving them money does.1

It would be a mistake to meet that gesture with anything but the specific credit it is due. Fifty thousand rand is a real sum, paid to real people, for real work: Themba Ndaba, Koleka Putuma, Gregory Maqoma, David Tlale and their fellow winners did not receive a symbolic honour this year. They received rent, or debt repayment, or the six months a working artist rarely gets to plan around. Mandoza's family received an additional hundred thousand rand, on top of his own Ministerial Award, nine years after his death — money that will matter to people who are still, in the ordinary sense, alive and paying for things. None of what follows is an argument that the money should not have been given, or that the artists who received it should feel anything but pleased to have it.

What the gesture is not, and what nothing in the department's own framing claims it to be, is a fix for the condition McKenzie was describing when he named it. "For thirty years, artists ... have been complaining" is, read carefully, an admission: the minister is not describing a communications failure. He is describing three decades in which the state's own cultural bureaucracy did not resource the people it claims to serve at the level they needed, consistently enough that the complaint became a known, named, recurring feature of the relationship between artists and the department. A single evening of cash prizes, however welcome, addresses that condition the way a bonus addresses an underpaid salary: it is real money, and it changes nothing about the structure that produced the underpayment in the first place.

A bonus is not a raise. Both put money in the same account. Only one of them changes what happens next year.

This publication has spent the better part of six months tracing what that structure actually looks like from the inside. The National Arts Council — the statutory body responsible for distributing public arts funding on an ongoing basis, as distinct from the department's own annual awards ceremony — has been through governance crises for most of its existence since 1997, each one disrupting the funding pipeline that practitioners depend on for the ordinary, unglamorous work of making things between ceremonies. This publication has documented, after the National Arts Festival closed in Makhanda in July, that South African theatre has no reliable mechanism for moving a well-reviewed fringe production to an audience beyond the festival that produced it — no touring fund, no shared calendar between the country's serious venues, nothing beyond the individual relationships of whichever maker happens to know a programmer elsewhere. This publication has tracked the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa's own eighteen-month search for a permanent director, a search that has now concluded but that left the institution's most significant contemporary art collection on the continent without a named curatorial authority for a year and a half. And this publication has followed Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy since its cancellation from the Venice pavilion in January, a work that exists in Venice this year not because the department funded an alternative once it withdrew its own support, but because the Bertha Foundation did.

None of these are separate stories from the awards ceremony at Sun City. They are the same department, in most cases the same minister, and in every case the same underlying question: does the state fund the infrastructure that makes cultural work possible on an ordinary Tuesday, or does it fund the ceremony that recognises the work after the fact, on television, once a year? A National Arts Council in recurring governance crisis and a National Arts and Culture Awards ceremony with a bigger prize pool are not competing priorities inside one budget. They are two different theories of what the department is for. The ceremony theory says: identify the best work already made, and reward it publicly. The infrastructure theory says: build the conditions under which more work, by more people, working in more places, becomes possible in the first place. A government can fund both. What this year's NACAs demonstrate is that the department currently knows how to do the first with real conviction, and has not, on the evidence this publication has gathered since March, shown the same conviction about the second.

Keorapetse Kgositsile's 1992 criticism — that the liberation movement itself had been "criminally backward when it comes to questions of culture" — was aimed at the ANC in exile, before the Constitution existed to make any of this a state responsibility at all.3 It is a harder charge than anything in this essay, made by someone who had given his life to the movement he was criticising, thirty-four years before a minister from that same political tradition stood at Sun City and said, in effect, that the criticism had finally been heard. There is a real distance between 1992 and 2026: a government that did not fund culture at all, and a government that now funds a televised prize evening with real cash attached. That distance is not nothing. But it is also not the distance Kgositsile was pointing toward. He was describing a movement that had not yet worked out what culture was for, structurally, inside its own theory of liberation. Thirty-four years and one democratic transition later, the department that inherited that unfinished thinking has worked out how to reward culture publicly. It has not yet, on this publication's own six months of tracking, worked out how to fund it privately, continuously, in the many rooms where no camera is present.

The distinction matters most, perhaps, in the case of the artists who cannot collect this year's cheque because they are dead. Mandoza, HHP, Don Mattera and Miriam Makeba were each honoured posthumously at Sun City — a form of recognition that costs a department nothing in future obligation, because the person being recognised will make no further demand on the budget. It is easier, always, to fund the past than the present: the dead do not apply for grants, do not need touring funds, do not sit on hold with a governance-crisis-stricken funding body waiting to hear whether this year's disbursement will arrive on schedule. What Fifty Thousand Rand buys, for the twenty living winners who received it, is real and should be named as such. What it does not buy is a answer to the question Kgositsile was actually asking in 1992: not whether culture would eventually be recognised, but whether the people building it would be resourced to keep building it, in the years between the ceremonies, when no one is watching.

Closing Reflection

Mduduzi Tshabalala died in 2016. His family received a hundred thousand rand at Sun City last week, on top of the standard Ministerial Award, because Minister McKenzie said Mandoza's story "cannot be forgotten." Nobody at that ceremony could have given him the money nine years ago, when a working musician still called Mandoza might have known what to do with it beyond honour his name.

That is not a criticism of the gesture to his family, who are owed whatever comfort it brings them. It is a fact about timing, and about which kind of artist a department finds it easiest to fund generously: the one who can no longer ask for anything else.
