The Less Good Idea: On William Kentridge, Institutional Freedom, and What Twenty-Three Months of Silence Costs

The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-third month without a named permanent director. Venice is eighty-two days into its run. In Maboneng, a different kind of institution — one built deliberately not to look like an institution — continues a decade of work that never required a board to decide anything. This week asks what the difference between the two teaches about what South African cultural infrastructure actually needs.

"Time was no longer a series of years, but a moment, holding all things ready for eternity."
— Bessie Head, A Question of Power, 1973

Thursday in South Africa, and it is the last Thursday of July. The National Arts Festival closed three weeks ago; the touring infrastructure question this publication raised in its wake has not been answered, because no institution exists whose job it is to answer it. Venice has been open eighty-two days — just over a third of its run, past the point where a Biennale's early reception hardens into the account that will be repeated until the closing. And in Cape Town, at the V&A Waterfront, the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa marks its twenty-third month without a named permanent director, a fact this publication has now tracked across eight consecutive editions without a single one of them reporting a change.

This week turns away from the institutions that are visibly struggling to decide, and toward one that decided, at its founding, not to be the kind of institution that has to. The Centre for the Less Good Idea opened in Maboneng, Johannesburg, in 2016, founded by William Kentridge and choreographer Bronwyn Lace as a space for artists to make work that does not need to justify itself to a board, a permanent collection, or an acquisitions committee. Its name comes from a Tswana proverb Kentridge has cited for years: if the good doctor cannot cure you, find the less good doctor. The premise is that the second attempt, made without the burden of having to be definitive, is often where the real thinking happens.

Kentridge turns seventy-one this year. He has spent five decades building an international reputation from a studio in Houghton, working in charcoal, print, tapestry, and film to make the same argument in different materials: that South African history is not behind us, that its erasures are still legible if you know how to look, and that the artist's job is to keep looking rather than to resolve what is seen into something comfortable. The Centre he built with Lace is, in this sense, not a departure from that project but its clearest institutional expression — an infrastructure built specifically to resist becoming the kind of institution that needs eighteen months to decide who runs it.

The essay this week takes up what that refusal costs and what it buys, and asks, directly, what it would mean for the Zeitz board to have read it as a model rather than an exception. Below: the month, the day count, a new literary date worth marking in your diary, and the Centre's current season.

Zeitz MOCAA: twenty-three months without a director. The Centre for the Less Good Idea: ten years without one being required. The difference is not resources, and it is not talent. It is what each institution decided, at its founding or in its drift, that permanence was for.

Curated Dispatches

Zeitz MOCAA — Twenty-Third Month

Cape Town  ·  V&A Waterfront Institutional Governance

The Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa enters its twenty-third month without a named permanent director. The pattern this publication has now documented across eight consecutive editions holds without variation: no public timeline, no public process description, no criteria disclosed to the sector the institution claims to serve. Venice's opening in May did not change it. The fiftieth anniversary of June 16 did not change it. The National Arts Festival's close, five weeks ago, did not change it.

What has changed, incrementally, is the weight the silence now carries. A board that takes eighteen months to appoint a successor is managing a difficult decision. A board that takes twenty-three is making one, whether or not it intends to: the decision that the institution can continue to function, publicly, without a named intelligence at its head, and that this is an acceptable state of affairs indefinitely. The curatorial staff who have sustained the programme since Koyo Kouoh's death deserve continued credit for the fact that the institution has not visibly deteriorated. That is not the same claim as the institution having been led.

Zeitz MOCAA, V&A Waterfront, Cape Town. Open Tuesday–Sunday. zeitzmocaa.museum.

Venice at Eighty-Two Days — The Middle of the Run

Arts Policy  ·  Venice Biennale Ongoing

The Venice Biennale has been open eighty-two days, a little over a third of its full run to 22 November. "In Minor Keys" has passed the point in a Biennale's life where the early critical excitement either compounds into a durable account or thins into the ordinary churn of a long exhibition season, and the record on Koyo Kouoh's posthumous exhibition has, if anything, deepened rather than faded: mid-run academic and critical writing is now engaging the exhibition's structure — the procession, the oasis, the school, the shrine — as a curatorial method worth teaching rather than a one-off gesture.

South Africa's position in that record has not moved and will not move before November. The absence is now simply part of what the Biennale is understood to have contained: the year its central exhibition was built by an African curatorial intelligence, and the year the country that produced her had nothing beside it in the Arsenale. Gabrielle Goliath's Elegy continues to be cited in critical accounts of the Biennale's periphery, work that found its audience without the pavilion the state declined to provide.

Venice Biennale Arte 2026. Open through 22 November 2026. labiennale.org.

The Centre for the Less Good Idea — Current Season, Maboneng

Maboneng, Johannesburg Ongoing

The Centre for the Less Good Idea's current season continues its founding format: short, intensely collaborative workshop periods that culminate in a small number of public performance evenings, mixing dance, music, film, and object-theatre in combinations that would not survive a conventional programming committee's risk assessment. The Centre does not maintain a permanent collection, does not seek to become a museum, and has said explicitly, through Kentridge and Lace's public statements over the past decade, that its ambition is the opposite of permanence — to remain small enough that failure is cheap and frequent, because failure cheaply borne is where the interesting work is found.

This is worth attending to specifically in the context this edition holds. The Centre is not a substitute for an institution like Zeitz MOCAA — it does not hold a collection, does not train the same audiences, does not carry the same custodial weight. But its governance model is a working counter-example to the assumption, implicit in the Zeitz board's eighteen months of silence, that institutional seriousness requires the deliberative caution of a large board managing a permanent asset. The Centre's seriousness is expressed differently: through speed, through the willingness to let work fail in public, through the refusal to let any single decision carry the weight of an entire institution's future.

The Centre for the Less Good Idea, Maboneng Precinct, Johannesburg. Programme at lessgoodidea.com.

Kingsmead Book Fair — Cape Town, Early August

Cape Town Advance Notice

The Kingsmead Book Fair returns to Cape Town in the first week of August — a smaller, more deliberately literary counterpart to the Franschhoek Literary Festival's winelands sprawl, held in a school hall rather than a wine estate, with a programme that has historically prioritised serious non-fiction, poetry, and debut fiction over the celebrity-author sessions that dominate the larger festival circuit. It is not a festival built for spectacle, and it has never tried to be.

Readers who found the Franschhoek programme's celebratory register more comfortable than analytical, as this publication noted in its 30 April edition, may find Kingsmead's smaller scale and more deliberate curation the better occasion for the kind of literary engagement this publication has been arguing South African festivals owe their more serious readers. Full programme details will be confirmed closer to the date; this notice is advance flagging only.

Kingsmead Book Fair, Cape Town. Early August 2026. Programme to be confirmed.

Long Essay

The Less Good Idea: On William Kentridge, Institutional Freedom, and What an Artist Builds When No Board Is Deciding For Him

In 2016, William Kentridge and Bronwyn Lace opened a small experimental workshop space in Maboneng, in downtown Johannesburg, and named it after a Tswana proverb: if the good doctor cannot cure you, find the less good doctor. The proverb is usually told as a piece of practical wisdom about persistence in the face of failure. Kentridge has told it, consistently, as something closer to an aesthetic philosophy — that the first attempt at anything, made with the full weight of expectation behind it, is rarely where the real discovery happens, and that the second attempt, made lighter, faster, and with permission to fail, is where an artist actually learns something new.

The Centre for the Less Good Idea has spent ten years operating on that premise. It runs short, intensive workshop periods — typically a matter of days — in which musicians, dancers, filmmakers, and visual artists are brought together with minimal brief and maximum permission to fail, culminating in a small run of public performances that are explicitly framed as work-in-progress rather than finished statement. The Centre holds no permanent collection. It seeks no museum-scale endowment. It has, by design, almost none of the institutional apparatus that a serious cultural institution is generally assumed to require: no acquisitions committee, no long-range collection strategy, no board deliberating for months over a single high-stakes appointment, because there is no single high-stakes appointment for a board to deliberate over. The Centre's continuity depends on Kentridge's continued involvement and reputation, which is its own kind of institutional fragility — but it is a fragility of a different order than the one currently on display at the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa, ninety minutes down the N1 in Cape Town.

The Zeitz MOCAA is, on paper, everything the Centre is not. It holds the largest dedicated collection of African contemporary art in the world. It occupies a Thomas Heatherwick-designed conversion of a nine-storey grain silo at the V&A Waterfront, a building whose architectural ambition alone generated years of international coverage before a single artwork was hung inside it. It was built, under Koyo Kouoh's leadership from 2019 until her death in May 2025, into an institutional argument of real intellectual seriousness: that African contemporary art's formal ambition and cosmological range demand infrastructure capable of holding them on their own epistemological terms, not infrastructure designed to make that art legible to a visiting international gaze. That argument reached its largest possible stage this May, when five curatorial advisers, working from Kouoh's archive, opened "In Minor Keys" as the central exhibition of the 61st Venice Biennale — the first time in the Biennale's history that its central exhibition had been conceived by an African-born curatorial intelligence working from an institutional home on the continent.

The Centre's seriousness is not expressed through permanence. It is expressed through the willingness to be wrong quickly, in public, and to call the result a season rather than a failure.

The institution that produced that argument is now, at the moment of the argument's largest public vindication, in its twenty-third month without a named person accountable for what comes next. This is not a minor administrative delay. Twenty-three months is longer than the full run of a Venice Biennale. It is longer than the National Arts Festival has existed in its current post-renaming form. It is a duration that has stopped being explicable as caution and has started to require its own explanation — not for what the board has decided, since it has apparently decided nothing publicly, but for what the board has evidently concluded is an acceptable cost of getting the decision right eventually, whenever eventually arrives.

The comparison between the Zeitz and the Centre for the Less Good Idea is not an argument that the Zeitz should have been built smaller, or that Kouoh's ambition for it was misplaced. A collection of the scale Zeitz MOCAA holds requires custodianship, security, conservation, and a scholarly infrastructure that a ten-day workshop model could never provide, and would be irresponsible to attempt. The comparison is narrower and more specific: it concerns not the scale of the institution but the culture of decision-making inside it, and whether that culture has confused the seriousness of the collection with the necessity of extended, unaccountable deliberation over who leads it.

Kentridge's institutional choice, at the Centre, was deliberate and specific: to build something whose governance could not seize up the way large boards seize up, because there was, structurally, very little for a board to seize up over. Each workshop period is bounded. Each failure is contained to a few days and a small public run, rather than compounding into an institution-wide crisis of confidence. This is not a model that scales to a collection like Zeitz's. But the principle underneath it — that an institution's capacity to act should not be held hostage to the magnitude of a single, infrequent decision — is a principle any institution can apply to its own governance design, regardless of scale. A museum board does not need to operate like a ten-day workshop. It does need to ask whether its appointment processes have been built to move, or built, inadvertently, to stall.

There is a version of this argument that collapses into a simple moral about small being better than large, agility being better than permanence. That is not the argument this essay is making, and it is not an argument the record supports. The Centre for the Less Good Idea has never held the responsibility of caring for a permanent collection worth hundreds of millions of rand, has never had to answer to the conservation and insurance and provenance obligations that come with that responsibility, and has never had to manage the expectations of an international art market watching to see who succeeds a curator of Kouoh's stature. Its freedom is real, and it is also a freedom bought by a specific, narrower set of obligations than the ones Zeitz MOCAA carries. The comparison is instructive, not because the two institutions are equivalent, but because the contrast makes visible a choice that the Zeitz board has not been forced to articulate: whether the scale of its custodial responsibility requires the scale of caution it has taken with the appointment, or whether the caution has become an end in itself, mistaken for diligence because no one has been willing to call it what it has become.

Kentridge is seventy-one this year. He has spent five decades making the argument, in charcoal and film and print, that South African history does not resolve — that its erasures remain legible to anyone willing to keep looking, and that the artist's discipline is to keep looking rather than accept the comfortable version. The Centre he built with Lace is that discipline given an institutional shape: a space that refuses permanence specifically so that the looking can continue, season after season, without the institution itself becoming the kind of settled thing that stops asking what it is for. Whether the Zeitz board understands that the same discipline — not smallness, but the refusal to let caution calcify into paralysis — is available to an institution of any size, is the question its twenty-third month of silence has not yet answered.

Closing Reflection

Bronwyn Lace has said, of the Centre's working method, that the point is never to arrive at the good idea. The point is to stay long enough in the less good one that something true gets said before anyone has decided what the finished version should look like. Kentridge is seventy-one. The Centre is ten. Neither fact resolves the other.

Twenty-three months is a long time to stay in a decision without saying anything true about it. The Zeitz board has not called its own silence a workshop. It has not called it anything. That is the difference the two institutions make visible, and it is not a difference this edition can settle. It can only keep naming it, month by month, until the board decides to.
