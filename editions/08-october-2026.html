On 23 September the Minister of Sport, Arts and Culture dissolved the council of the National Film and Video Foundation, the second time in two years. The department’s own statement names a cost of the last dissolution: three months of stalled funding approvals. This edition asks who carries an interval like that, and corrects an error of its own about the National Arts Council. Thursday in South Africa, and the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa’s new Artistic Director is a week into her post. Gabrielle Goliath’s Elegy opens today at Ibraaz in London, as the last edition said it would. Both threads are in the tracker below and neither is the subject of this edition, because each was covered last week and the rule is that a subject is rested before it returns.

The subject is a notice that did not make the cultural pages. On 23 September, according to a statement from the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture published on 1 October, Minister Gayton McKenzie dissolved the council of the National Film and Video Foundation, the statutory body through which public money reaches South African film.1 It is the second time. The foundation’s previous council was dissolved in October 2024.3 Between them sits a third episode this publication has followed, the dissolution of the National Arts Council in late May and its suspension by the High Court at the end of July.4

Edition 27, on 3 September, treated the National Arts Council episode as part of a pattern of appointments and complaints. This edition takes a narrower and more testable angle: the instrument itself, and its cost. A dissolution is a legal act with a start date. What it leaves behind is an interval, the time between the act and a council that can approve anything. The department’s statement is unusually direct that this interval has a price, and says what the price was last time.1

The weekend’s other business is smaller and sits at the other end of the same pipeline. The Joburg Underground Film Festival runs on Saturday and Sunday at the Bioscope, and the Market Theatre opens a new play on Thursday 15 October that will anchor the theatre’s first Queer Festival.57 No book could be verified inside the seven-day window, so none is featured; the brief calls for one, and this edition prefers an empty slot to an unconfirmed one.

A correction precedes the dispatches. It concerns the National Arts Council, and it is the kind of error that compounds if it is not named: a number that was repeated, not checked.

Joburg Underground Film Festival — Two Days at the Bioscope

Johannesburg 10–11 October Single source

The Joburg Underground Film Festival returns to the Bioscope on Saturday 10 and Sunday 11 October, according to TimesLive’s October guide, which lists a ticket price of R104.50.5 The Bioscope is a small independent cinema, and an underground festival there is a setting in which films that fall outside a funding body’s categories can be shown to a paying audience.

The editorial reason it is here is the week’s argument, stated with its limit. This edition does not know how any film in the programme was financed, and does not claim that this festival is a product of, or a response to, the foundation’s dissolution. The sources read for it confirm the dates, the venue and the price, and nothing about the programme. It is listed because the pipeline of funded film and the circuit of independent screening share an audience, and because a week in which the funder has no council is a reasonable week to look at the screens that do not depend on one.

The Bioscope, Johannesburg. Saturday 10 and Sunday 11 October 2026. Ticket R104.50 per TimesLive guide; confirm programme and times at thebioscope.co.za.

The Breasts of Tiresias — Market Theatre, From Thursday

Newtown, Johannesburg Opens 15 October To 25 October Cross-checked

The Market Theatre opens The Breasts of Tiresias next Thursday, 15 October, for a strictly limited run to 25 October, directed by Malcolm Purkey, with Gerard Bester and Sandi Dlangalala in the cast and live music.57 The theatre describes some of the performances as part of its inaugural Queer Festival, which a press guide dates to 21–25 October.7 The title is also that of Guillaume Apollinaire’s 1917 play; whether this production adapts it is not stated in the sources read.

The institutional fact is worth setting beside this week’s essay. The Market Theatre first opened its doors in June 1976, according to its own account, and the Market Theatre Foundation is an agency of the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture, as this publication noted in Edition 24.7 A state-linked theatre inaugurating a queer festival in the department’s fiftieth-year-plus-one season is a programming decision, and this edition reports it as one. What it cannot yet report is how the festival was funded or who approved it, because neither is in the sources.

Long Essay

The Interval: On a Council Dissolved Twice in Two Years, and What Happens Between the Notice and the Quorum

On 23 September 2026, according to the Department of Sport, Arts and Culture, Minister Gayton McKenzie dissolved the council of the National Film and Video Foundation under section 6(10) of the Act that established it.1 It was the second dissolution of that council in two years. The first came in October 2024, when the acting chief executive was also placed on administrative leave.3 The department’s statement, published on 1 October, contains the sentence on which this essay turns: it acknowledges that when the council was last dissolved, funding approvals stalled for about three months.1

That sentence is rare in the documents of this sector, and it should be read for what it is. It is a minister’s own estimate of the cost of the instrument he has just used again. The essay does not argue that the estimate is wrong, or that the dissolution was unjustified. It argues that the estimate is the only measurement of the interval that exists in the sources, and that nobody is charged with updating it.

The sequence this year is short and is drawn from the documents.

  1. October 2024The foundation’s council is dissolved and the acting chief executive placed on administrative leave.3
  2. September 2025GroundUp reports on leadership instability at the foundation, under the headline that the national film foundation “falters”.10
  3. Late May 2026The Minister dissolves the National Arts Council. At the end of July the High Court suspends the dissolution pending judicial review and reinstates the council.4
  4. 23 September 2026The Minister dissolves the foundation’s council again. The department’s statement says existing funding commitments will be honoured and that a new council will be appointed.1

Three dissolutions across two bodies in twenty-three months is a pattern, but the pattern should not be mistaken for a verdict. The foundation’s own record is documented and uncomfortable: the 2024 dissolution followed an administrative suspension, and the 2025 reporting describes a body struggling to hold its leadership.310 A reader who concludes that the council was blameless would be reading beyond the evidence. A reader who concludes that dissolution is therefore a remedy would be reading beyond it in the other direction. The sources support a narrower claim: the instrument has been used three times, and the body it was used on has not stabilised afterwards.

A statutory buffer is as independent as removing it is expensive, and the expense lands on the applicants, not on the person who signs the notice.

The structural point is about who pays. A council that is dissolved stops approving grants. The department’s statement says commitments already made continue to be honoured, which protects the filmmakers who have contracts.1 It leaves unanswered what happens to the ones who do not: the applicant whose proposal was in the queue on 22 September, the producer budgeting a shoot against an approval that has now moved by an unknown number of weeks. The sources do not say how new applications are handled in the interval. That silence is the finding. An interval with a known price and no published count is an interval nobody is accountable for.

The National Arts Council episode shows where the check actually sat. The dissolution of that body was suspended not by a statutory process inside the department but by a court, after the fact, at the instance of the people dissolved. One report describes the reinstated council as having 24 members; the eleven this publication reported in Edition 27 appear to have been the applicants who went to court.46 The correction at the top of this edition withdraws that number. It also sharpens the point. The institution that could stop a dissolution was a judge, the mechanism was litigation, and the party who had to start it was the party who had just lost its seat.

That arrangement has a particular consequence for funded work. A dissolved council can sue. An applicant waiting on a council cannot, because the applicant has no standing in the dispute and no contract yet. Whatever remedy exists runs through the people with seats, and those people have both a legitimate grievance and an interest. This is not a criticism of them. It is a description of whose interests the existing remedy can express and whose it cannot.

This edition has not read the foundation’s response to the notice, and it has not read the statutes’ provisions on interim arrangements. It does not claim that those provisions are silent. It claims only that no source it read reports a number, for any of the three dissolutions, other than the minister’s three months, and that this number concerns the last interval and not the present one.

The unanswered question is therefore measurable. How many days will pass between 23 September 2026 and the first meeting of a new foundation council that can approve a grant, who will publish the count, and will it be longer or shorter than the three months the department has already admitted?

Closing Reflection

The department’s statement contains one number about what a dissolution costs. It is three months, it belongs to the last time, and it was written by the person who has just signed the notice for the second time. No source read for this edition gives a number for this interval, or for the one before it at the National Arts Council.

Somewhere in that interval is a proposal that was in a queue on 22 September. Its author does not appear in any document this edition read, and has no seat on any council. The count has started, and nobody has said who is counting.
